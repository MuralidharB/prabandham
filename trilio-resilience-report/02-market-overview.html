Market Overview & Definitions

Three disciplines collapsing into one market, and the five archetypes that supply it.

Key facts
  • Public 2026 market estimates span ~$7B to ~$16B — the spread reflects scope definitions, not disagreement about direction
  • Growth concentrates at the edges: BaaS/DRaaS, SaaS-app, Kubernetes and cyber-resilience segments outpace the ~10% headline
  • Spend per protected terabyte is falling while spend per organization rises
  • Modernization is triggered by shocks — a ransomware near-miss, an insurance renewal, an audit finding — not refresh cycles

This section establishes the report’s terminology, draws the scope boundaries that later analysis depends on, and sketches the market’s size, growth, and supply-side structure. Definitional care is not pedantry here: the industry’s central commercial dynamic is precisely that three once-distinct categories — backup, disaster recovery, and cyber resilience — are converging, and much of the confusion in vendor messaging and buyer requirements traces to the blurred lines between them.

2.1Three Disciplines, One Converging Market

Backup and recovery is the discipline of maintaining restorable point-in-time copies of data and systems. Its defining characteristics are version depth (a history of recovery points across days, weeks, or years) and granularity (restore a file, a mailbox, a table, or a full system). Its historical purpose was protection against error, corruption, and component failure; its unit of success is the restore.

Disaster recovery (DR) is the discipline of resuming operations after the loss of a site, region, or platform. Its defining characteristics are continuity rather than depth — standby capacity, replication, orchestrated failover — and its unit of success is the resumed service. DR strategies are specified by two metrics that recur throughout this report: recovery time objective (RTO), the tolerable duration of outage, and recovery point objective (RPO), the tolerable window of data loss. Backup answers “can we get the data back?”; DR answers “how fast are we running again, and from how recent a state?”

Cyber resilience is the newest and most contested term: the capacity to withstand, contain, and recover from a deliberate attack — most saliently ransomware — rather than an accident. It inherits from backup (clean historical copies), from DR (rapid resumption), and from security operations (detection, forensics, incident response), and adds requirements native to none of them: immutability against hostile administrators, isolated copies outside the compromised trust domain, validation that restored data is actually clean, and recovery into environments that are not themselves crime scenes.

The convergence logic, developed fully in Section 4, is that a targeted adversary collapses the distinctions: a ransomware event is simultaneously a data-integrity incident (needing backup’s version depth), an availability disaster (needing DR’s orchestration), and a security breach (needing forensic discipline). The market is reorganizing around that collapsed scenario — which is why this report treats the three disciplines as one industry with internal specializations, and why its scope statement is: technologies and services whose purpose is the restorability of enterprise data and the recoverability of enterprise operations, across accidental and adversarial causes.

Adjacent categories sit outside scope except where they intersect: primary storage resilience (RAID, array replication) enters only as capture mechanism (Section 6.2); archiving for information governance enters only where retention infrastructure is shared; broader security operations enter only at the convergence seam.

2.2Market Size and Growth

Definitional convergence makes market sizing genuinely ambiguous — analyst estimates differ substantially depending on whether they count software only, appliances, BaaS/DRaaS service revenue, and the cyber-resilience components increasingly funded from security budgets. Directionally, the picture is consistent across estimates:

  • Data-protection software and platforms constitute a market in the low-to-mid tens of billions of dollars annually, growing at high single to low double digits — respectable but unremarkable. Public market trackers illustrate the definitional spread directly: estimates for 2026 range from roughly $7 billion under narrow backup-and-recovery definitions to around $16 billion under broader data-protection scopes, with growth estimates clustering near 10% annually and stretching toward 20% for the widest definitions.1
  • Growth is concentrated at the edges, not the core. The as-a-service segment (BaaS/DRaaS), SaaS-application protection, Kubernetes/cloud-native protection, and cyber-resilience components are each growing at multiples of the headline rate — public DRaaS forecasts alone cluster from the mid-teens to above 20% annually — while traditional on-premises license revenue is flat to declining. The aggregate figure therefore understates how quickly the market’s composition is changing.
  • Spend per protected terabyte is falling; spend per organization is rising. Storage commoditization (Section 6) pushes unit economics down while surface expansion, retention lengthening, and resilience requirements push total budgets up — a scissors pattern that explains much of the vendor repositioning documented later.

For strategy purposes, the composition shifts matter more than the headline number: they identify where pricing power is draining (single-platform basics, raw repository capacity) and where it is pooling (cross-platform consistency, evidence, resilience, and the white space of Section 9.3).

1

2.3The Supply Side: An Archetype Taxonomy

Consistent with this report’s method, the vendor landscape is mapped as five structural archetypes rather than named companies. They are introduced here for use throughout the report and analyzed in depth in Section 9:

  1. Legacy incumbents modernizing — platforms born in the physical and early-virtual eras; deepest workload breadth and installed bases; re-platforming toward services and cyber resilience.
  2. Cloud-native platforms — SaaS-delivered, object-storage-based, API-first; strongest on the modern estate; structurally challenged by sovereignty.
  3. Hyperscaler-native services — the cloud providers’ own offerings; frictionless within their platform, absent beyond it; function as the market’s price floor.
  4. Security-converged players — positioned on cyber resilience and CISO budgets; deep on crown-jewel protection, thinner on estate breadth.
  5. MSP and BaaS specialists — operated services, often on others’ technology; the practical vehicle for mid-market delivery and sovereign operation.

Two features of this structure shape everything downstream. First, the archetypes’ strengths are complementary rather than overlapping — breadth, modernity, integration, security depth, and operational proximity sit in different corners — which is why no single archetype currently satisfies the full requirement set (Section 9.2) and why enterprise estates typically span more than one. Second, the boundaries are active: incumbents acquiring cloud-native capability, security platforms acquiring protection assets, hyperscalers absorbing basics — the convergence in motion that Section 10’s scenarios extrapolate.

2.4The Demand Side in Brief

Buyer structure previews the fuller treatment in Section 8. The enterprise segment is defined by heterogeneous estates (hence the consolidation-versus-exception tension), regulatory exposure (hence evidence and sovereignty requirements), and dual budget ownership between infrastructure and security organizations. The mid-market is defined by scarce operational capability, making it the natural BaaS/DRaaS constituency and the segment where hyperscaler-native and MSP archetypes compete most directly. Across both, the buying event has shifted: modernization projects are triggered less by refresh cycles than by external shocks — a ransomware near-miss, an insurance renewal, an audit finding, a regulatory deadline — a pattern with direct implications for how vendors time and frame their go-to-market.

2.5Section Takeaways

The market is best understood as three disciplines collapsing into one under adversarial pressure, sized in the tens of billions but repricing internally at speed, supplied by five structurally distinct archetypes whose gaps do not overlap, and bought increasingly in response to shocks rather than cycles. With terms and structure established, the report turns to the forces driving the change — beginning with the infrastructure landscape.
