Sovereignty & Data Regulation

Residency, jurisdiction and operational control — a durable architectural constraint.

Key facts
  • Sovereignty is three distinct layers — residency, jurisdiction, operational control — with very different architectural costs
  • Backup catalogs, control planes and support access are in scope, not just the data
  • Customer-held encryption keys are the highest-leverage jurisdictional control
  • The sovereign segment grows faster than the market — and is structurally protected from unwilling vendors

Sovereignty has moved from a European policy debate to a global architectural constraint. Governments increasingly treat data location, legal jurisdiction, and operational control as matters of national interest, and enterprises — particularly in regulated sectors and the public sector — are inheriting those requirements through procurement rules, regulation, and risk policy. Because backup and disaster recovery by definition create additional copies of data, often in different locations, data protection sits directly in the path of every sovereignty requirement. This section examines what sovereignty actually demands, where it is being mandated, and what it means for both sides of the market.

5.1What Sovereignty Actually Means: Three Distinct Layers

Sovereignty discussions frequently conflate three requirements with very different architectural consequences. Precision matters, because vendors market against all three and buyers must know which they are actually obligated to meet.

Data residency is the narrowest claim: data is stored and processed within a defined geography. Most hyperscalers can satisfy residency through regional data centers, and most backup platforms can pin repositories to a region. Residency alone, however, does not address who can access the data or under whose laws.

Jurisdictional sovereignty concerns legal exposure: whether a foreign government can compel access to data regardless of where it physically sits. Extraterritorial instruments — most prominently U.S. legislation permitting compelled disclosure from U.S.-headquartered providers — mean that data residing in a European region of an American hyperscaler remains, in the view of many European regulators and courts, subject to foreign jurisdiction. This is the layer that residency marketing cannot solve and the one driving the most consequential architecture decisions.

Operational sovereignty is the strictest tier: the personnel who operate the infrastructure, the support chain, the encryption key custody, and in some frameworks even the software supply chain must be under local or trusted control. Requirements at this level typically demand locally owned operating entities, customer-held or nationally held encryption keys, and cleared local staff.

For data protection specifically, these layers translate into concrete questions: Where do backup copies — including replicas, cloud tiers, and DR targets — physically reside? Who holds the keys to encrypted backups? Can the backup vendor’s foreign support organization access customer data or metadata? Where does the control plane run — a frequently missed detail, since many SaaS-delivered backup platforms process metadata, catalogs, and sometimes indexes in regions different from the data itself. Backup catalogs and metadata can reveal filenames, user identities, and organizational structure; several regulators have signaled that metadata is not exempt.

5.2The Regional Landscape

Europe remains the center of gravity. GDPR established the baseline for personal-data transfers, but the momentum now comes from sector and resilience regulation: DORA — applicable since January 2025 — imposes ICT risk-management, third-party oversight, and tested recovery obligations on financial entities, with explicit attention to concentration risk in cloud providers, and entered its first genuine supervisory enforcement cycle in 2026, with regulators signaling action on incident-reporting failures and deficiencies in the mandatory register of ICT third-party arrangements. NIS2 extends resilience and incident-reporting duties across essential and important entities in a broad set of sectors; its national transposition has been slow — the Commission opened infringement procedures against 23 member states in late 2024, and roughly 20 had adopted national laws by mid-2026 — with compliance deadlines converging around October 2026.1 Layered on top are certification and procurement frameworks — national schemes in France and elsewhere requiring immunity from non-EU law for sensitive workloads, and the long-running EU-level certification saga: the draft EU cloud certification scheme (EUCS) originally proposed sovereignty criteria for its highest assurance level (EU headquarters, immunity from non-EU law), removed them in 2024 under member-state and industry pressure in favor of a jurisdiction-transparency attestation, and remains unadopted as of mid-2026 amid active calls to reinstate them.1 The European Commission has meanwhile begun applying its own Cloud Sovereignty Framework — which scores providers on sovereignty criteria — to its internal procurement, and the Cybersecurity Act review now underway keeps the question open. Hyperscalers have responded with sovereign-cloud offerings operated through European partners or legally separated local entities; European providers position sovereignty as their core differentiator.

The Middle East has moved fast and prescriptively. Gulf states have enacted data-protection laws with residency expectations for government and critical-sector data, paired with national cloud strategies and large sovereign investments in local data-center capacity. Government procurement frequently requires in-country hosting and locally licensed providers, making local presence a condition of market entry rather than an optimization.

Asia-Pacific is heterogeneous but trends the same direction: strict data-localization and cross-border transfer regimes in some markets, sector-specific residency rules for financial and government data in others, and public-cloud procurement frameworks that certify providers at graded assurance levels. India, Indonesia, Vietnam, and others have adopted or expanded localization requirements; Australia and Japan emphasize certified hosting for government workloads.

The direction of travel is consistent worldwide: more classes of data, in more sectors, in more countries, carrying location and control constraints — and enforcement growing teeth.

NIS2: Directive (EU) 2022/2555, transposition deadline October 2024, widely missed; enforcement regimes going live through 2026. 5.3Implications for Enterprise Buyers

Sovereignty converts backup architecture from a technical design into a compliance artifact. Practical consequences:

Map obligations to data classes, not to the whole estate. Few organizations need operational sovereignty for everything. The workable pattern is classification — which datasets carry residency or jurisdictional constraints — followed by tiered architecture: sovereign-grade protection for the constrained minority, standard architecture for the rest. Over-applying sovereignty requirements inflates cost dramatically.

Interrogate the full copy chain. Compliance reviews routinely clear primary systems and miss the DR replica in a foreign region, the long-term retention tier in a hyperscaler archive, or the vendor’s telemetry pipeline. Every copy, every catalog, every support-access path is in scope.

Key custody is the highest-leverage control. Customer-held encryption keys, managed in-country and inaccessible to the provider, mitigate much of the jurisdictional exposure even on foreign-owned infrastructure — and regulators increasingly recognize this. Buyers should weight external key management and bring-your-own-key support heavily in evaluations.

Watch concentration risk. DORA-style regimes make dependence on a single foreign provider itself a reportable risk. Cross-provider recovery capability — the portability theme from Section 3 — doubles as a sovereignty and concentration-risk answer.

5.4Implications for Vendor Strategy

Sovereignty is fragmenting what was becoming a globally uniform SaaS delivery model, and vendors face real portfolio decisions:

Deployment flexibility is back. The industry’s decade-long march toward SaaS-only control planes collides with buyers who cannot accept a foreign-operated control plane. Vendors able to offer self-hosted, dedicated-region, or partner-operated control planes — without forking the product — address a market that pure-SaaS competitors must concede. This is a genuine differentiation axis identified in the Section 9 archetype analysis.

Certifications are market-entry tickets. National scheme qualifications, local assurance certifications, and government-procurement listings function as hard gates, not marketing. The investment is country-by-country and ongoing; vendors must choose target jurisdictions deliberately rather than pursuing all of them.

Partnership is often the only viable structure. Requirements for locally owned operators favor models in which the vendor supplies technology and a local partner — telco, national systems integrator, or sovereign-cloud operator — delivers the service. Vendors comfortable with white-label and managed-service economics gain reach; those insisting on direct relationships lock themselves out of prescriptive markets.

Sell the metadata story. Vendors that can demonstrate — not merely assert — that catalogs, indexes, telemetry, and support access respect the same boundaries as the data itself will find this increasingly decisive in regulated evaluations.

5.5Section Takeaways

Sovereignty is not a passing regulatory mood; it is a durable rebalancing of how governments treat data infrastructure, and its requirements land with particular force on data protection because copies, catalogs, and control planes are precisely what the discipline creates. Buyers should respond with classification and tiering rather than blanket sovereignty, and should treat key custody and copy-chain mapping as the core diligence items. Vendors should treat deployment flexibility, certification strategy, and local partnership models as first-order portfolio decisions — the sovereign segment is growing faster than the market average, and it is structurally protected from competitors unwilling to make those investments.
