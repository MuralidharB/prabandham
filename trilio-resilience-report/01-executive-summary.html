Executive Summary

The findings in brief — and what they demand of buyers and vendors.

Key facts
  • Three disciplines — backup, DR, cyber resilience — are collapsing into one market under adversarial pressure
  • Five vendor archetypes supply the market; none clears the full requirement bar
  • The 2026–2030 outlook: five baseline certainties, three genuinely open scenarios
  • One thesis: this market now rewards proof and punishes assumption

The backup, recovery, and disaster recovery industry is being rebuilt around a single scenario it was never originally designed for: a targeted, intelligent adversary who plans for the existence of backups and attacks them first. That inversion — from insurance against accidents to active defense against ransomware — is reorganizing the market’s technology, economics, buying centers, and competitive structure simultaneously. This report maps those forces for two audiences: enterprise buyers deciding how to architect and procure resilience, and vendor strategy teams deciding where to play.

1.1The Findings in Brief

Three disciplines are collapsing into one market. Backup (version depth), disaster recovery (continuity), and cyber resilience (surviving deliberate attack) were once separable. A ransomware incident is all three at once, and requirements, budgets, and products are converging around that collapsed scenario (Section 2). The market’s headline growth is unremarkable, but its composition is repricing fast: value is draining from single-platform basics and raw capacity toward cross-platform consistency, security depth, and evidence (Sections 2, 6).

The protection surface has fragmented beyond any single tool’s reach. Hyperscaler estates, hybrid infrastructure, Kubernetes and stateful containers, SaaS application data, edge fleets, and now AI pipelines each break assumptions of the previous era’s tooling. Native hyperscaler services set a rising floor for single-cloud basics but stop at their platform’s edge — leaving cross-platform policy, isolation from a compromised account, and recovery mobility as the durable third-party value (Section 3).

The threat landscape is the market’s primary buying driver. Modern ransomware operations neutralize recovery capability before detonating; extortion economics mean backup fully solves only the encryption leg of an attack. The architectural response — storage-enforced immutability, isolated trust domains, clean-room recovery, last-known-clean identification — has moved from advanced practice toward table stakes, while cyber insurance and operational-resilience regulation convert best practice into hard requirement. The bar has shifted from having backups to proving recoverability (Section 4).

Sovereignty is a durable architectural constraint, not a passing policy mood. Residency, jurisdictional exposure, and operational control are distinct requirements with different consequences; backup sits directly in their path because copies, catalogs, and control planes are precisely what the discipline creates. The sovereign segment is growing faster than the market and is structurally protected from vendors unwilling to invest in flexible deployment, certification, and local partnership (Section 5).

Storage economics set the ceiling on every strategic ambition. Object storage has become the default repository and is commoditizing it; flash is bought for restore speed, tape is rehabilitated as the physical air gap; and the real cost of retention is a round trip — the price of restoring at incident scale, which routinely shocks organizations that optimized only for storage at rest (Section 6).

AI enters twice. As capability, detection features are table stakes, assistants are the competitive front, and autonomous recovery is emerging with governance requirements to match. As workload, training data, model checkpoints, vector stores, and pipeline configuration form a new protection surface that no vendor archetype yet serves coherently — the clearest white space in the market (Section 7).

Buying has become bigger, more senior, and evidence-driven. BaaS/DRaaS and consumption pricing keep gaining share; budget ownership is migrating partly to security organizations; consolidation pressure is real but wins by default rather than universally — the pragmatic enterprise pattern is a primary platform plus deliberate, justified exceptions, including an independent copy of last resort. The evaluation criteria that actually differentiate are the ones requiring demonstration: timed restores at scale, compromised-administrator exercises, automated validation with audit-grade evidence (Section 8).

No vendor archetype clears the full bar. Across the five structural archetypes — legacy incumbents modernizing, cloud-native platforms, hyperscaler-native services, security-converged players, and MSP/BaaS specialists — strengths are complementary rather than overlapping: breadth, modernity, integration, security depth, and operational proximity sit in different corners. Every consolidation decision is therefore a choice of which gaps to accept, and archetypes are converging in messaging far faster than in architecture (Section 9).

The 2026–2030 outlook is a demanding baseline plus three open questions. Certain in every scenario: evidence-grade recoverability, universal attacked-backup assumptions, service delivery growth, sovereignty spread, and continued surface expansion. Genuinely open: whether security platforms absorb the category, whether hyperscalers commoditize the base layer, and how far sovereign fragmentation runs. The probable outcome is a blend that resolves differently by segment — with specific signposts identified for each (Section 10).

1.2What Buyers Should Do

Design for the attacked-backup assumption — hostile-administrator-proof immutability, separate trust domains, backup infrastructure off the production identity plane. Tier the estate by criticality and regulatory constraint under one policy engine rather than protecting uniformly. Procure on demonstration, not attestation: witnessed restores at scale, contracted recovery commitments, growth-capped pricing. Cost the recovery round trip before signing, not during an incident. Consolidate with intent while keeping deliberate exceptions, including an independently sourced last-resort copy. Bring infrastructure and security budget owners in together from the start. Inventory and tier the AI estate before it outgrows its absent protection policy. And use the regulatory and insurance wave as funding for the evidence tooling a well-run program needs anyway. (Section 11a.)

1.3Where Vendors Should Play

Fix the archetype gap that is existential, not the one that is embarrassing — each archetype has a different survival move, and strategies that rank all gaps equally have not decided anything. Assume the hyperscaler floor rises annually and concentrate differentiation above it: cross-platform mobility, independent trust, compromised-account recovery, compliance-grade evidence. Move early on the white space — AI-pipeline protection most urgently, where category credibility is still unclaimed. Make evidence the flagship product, because provable recoverability is the report’s safest bet and the hardest capability to commoditize. Architect for the full sovereignty spectrum rather than a sovereign checkbox. Set channel economics deliberately — marketplaces, MSPs, and GSIs now shape competitive position as much as product. Sell to the converged buying center with precision rather than overclaim. And instrument the scenario signposts with pre-agreed responses, because the three open questions resolve differently by segment and early recognition is worth quarters. (Section 11b.)

1.4The Thesis in One Line

This market now rewards proof and punishes assumption: proven recovery for buyers, provable differentiation for vendors — and the participants who internalize that shift earliest will own the next phase of a category whose strategic importance has never been higher.
