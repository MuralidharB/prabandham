Chapter 10 of 11

Outlook & Scenarios 2026–2030

A demanding baseline, three open questions, and the signposts that will decide them.

Key facts
  • Five baseline trends hold in every plausible future — plan on them, don’t debate them
  • Three open questions: who owns the budget, who owns the base layer, who may operate where
  • The probable 2030 is a blend that resolves differently by segment
  • Each scenario has concrete signposts — instrument them rather than watching passively

Forecasting this market as a single trajectory would misrepresent it: several of the forces documented in this report could each, if it runs to its logical end, reorganize the industry around a different center of gravity. This section therefore separates what is effectively certain — the baseline trends that hold in every plausible future — from three structural scenarios whose resolution is genuinely open, and closes with the signposts that will indicate which way the market is breaking.

10.1The Baseline: Trends That Hold in Every Scenario

Five developments are sufficiently locked in by regulation, threat economics, and installed momentum that strategy should treat them as givens rather than bets:

Recoverability becomes an evidence discipline. The regulatory arc of Sections 4 and 5 — operational-resilience regimes, tested-recovery mandates, incident reporting — continues to spread across sectors and jurisdictions. By the end of the decade, “we back up” is no more acceptable to an auditor than “we have a firewall”; the deliverable is demonstrated, documented recovery within stated tolerances. Platforms without automated verification and audit-grade evidence will not be shortlisted in regulated industries.

The attacked-backup assumption becomes universal. Immutability, isolation, and compromised-scenario design — currently differentiators in enterprise deals — complete their migration to table stakes, following the path immutability itself has already traveled. Adversary attention moves accordingly, up the stack to identity planes, management consoles, and (as autonomy spreads) the AI operating the recovery infrastructure itself.

Consumption and service delivery keep gaining share. The BaaS/DRaaS and consumption-pricing shifts of Section 8 continue across every scenario; what varies is who captures the service margin.

Sovereignty requirements broaden rather than recede. More countries, more sectors, more data classes. The fragmentation cost this imposes on global delivery models is a permanent feature of the planning horizon.

The protection surface keeps expanding faster than tooling consolidates. AI workloads are the current frontier (Section 7.2); they will not be the last.

10.2Scenario One: Absorption into the Security Platform

The premise. Cyber resilience completes its migration into the security category. Backup telemetry becomes a standard security-operations data source, recovery becomes the final stage of every incident-response playbook, and the major security platforms — through acquisition and build — absorb data protection as a module, much as they absorbed adjacent categories before it. Budget ownership consolidates under the CISO; standalone backup vendors face the fate of standalone antivirus.

The mechanics that drive it. The budget migration of Section 8.2 accelerates; security platforms’ M&A capacity dwarfs the data-protection sector’s market capitalization; boards, conditioned by ransomware, find “one resilience platform” narratively irresistible.

What restrains it. Infrastructure-grade backup breadth is genuinely hard — the incumbent archetype’s decades of workload connectors are not acquirable in one transaction, and security platforms have historically struggled to operate infrastructure-heavy products. The estate-breadth constraint that keeps security-converged players a second platform (Section 9.1) applies to acquirers too. And the monoculture-isolation argument of Section 8.3 cuts directly against consolidation into the same platform an adversary targets first.

If it wins: buyers gain integration and lose negotiating leverage; the deliberate-exception, independent last-copy pattern becomes the critical architectural safeguard. Vendor implication: standalone players need either security-platform-grade detection credibility or acquisition-ready positioning.

10.3Scenario Two: Hyperscaler Commoditization of the Base Layer

The premise. The floor-setting dynamic of Section 9.1 extends upward. Hyperscalers, motivated by storage revenue and workload lock-in, invest their way from single-cloud basics into credible immutable vaults, cross-region isolation, and eventually cross-cloud protection — collapsing the price of the base layer to a cloud-bill line item. Third-party value concentrates entirely in the thin orchestration, evidence, and intelligence layer above; the industry’s revenue pool contracts even as its workload coverage grows.

The mechanics that drive it. Marketplace procurement gravity; committed-spend draw-down economics that make “good enough and already paid for” the default; the same up-stack compression already visible at the storage layer (Section 6.1).

What restrains it. The trust asymmetry — same-provider protection is structurally weaker isolation, and every major cloud identity-compromise incident re-teaches the lesson; sovereignty regimes that treat U.S. hyperscaler expansion as the problem, not the solution; and hyperscalers’ consistent historical reluctance to invest deeply in protecting competitors’ platforms, which caps the cross-cloud ambition.

If it wins: buyers enjoy falling base-layer prices but must fund the independent-isolation layer deliberately, as a security control rather than an infrastructure default. Vendor implication: pricing power survives only in what natives cannot offer — cross-platform consistency, independent trust domains, compliance evidence, and the white-space items of Section 9.3.

10.4Scenario Three: Sovereign Fragmentation

The premise. The sovereignty trajectory of Section 5 steepens — driven by geopolitical deterioration, a landmark extraterritorial-access case, or aggressive enforcement of localization regimes — until the global data-protection market decomposes into regional markets with distinct certification regimes, local operating requirements, and limited cross-recognition. Global platform economics break; regional champions and locally partnered delivery models take share structurally.

The mechanics that drive it. Every incentive currently points this direction for governments; none point back. The EU certification debate, Gulf procurement rules, and APAC localization laws are all live vectors.

What restrains it. Cost. Full fragmentation is expensive for every participant including the states imposing it, and the historical pattern is negotiated accommodation — sovereign variants, trusted-partner structures, key-custody compromises — rather than clean decomposition.

If it wins: the incumbents’ self-hosted heritage and the MSP archetype’s local structures (the nonobvious sovereignty leaders of Section 9.2) become decisive advantages; pure-global SaaS becomes a mid-market and unregulated-sector model. Buyer implication: multinational enterprises carry permanent multi-standard protection estates, and the classification-and-tiering discipline of Section 5.3 becomes the core architecture skill.

10.5The Probable Blend and the Signposts

These scenarios are not mutually exclusive, and the most probable 2030 is a blend: security absorption progressing fastest in the mid-market and at the crown-jewel tier; hyperscaler commoditization capturing the single-cloud base layer while independent platforms hold the cross-platform and evidence layers; sovereignty fragmenting the regulated segment while the unregulated global market consolidates. The strategic question for any participant is not which scenario is true but which one dominates their segment.

Signposts to watch, by scenario. Security absorption: a top-tier security platform acquiring a top-tier data-protection vendor (the unambiguous trigger); CISO-owned share of protection budgets crossing the majority line; incident-response retainers bundling recovery execution. Hyperscaler commoditization: any hyperscaler shipping credible protection for a competitor’s cloud; marketplace share of protection procurement passing direct sales; native-service pricing folded entirely into compute commitments. Sovereign fragmentation: sovereignty criteria surviving into the highest tier of the EU certification scheme; a precedent-setting extraterritorial data-access ruling; a major economy mandating locally operated protection for a broad sector rather than a narrow one.

10.6Section Takeaways

The baseline is demanding but plannable: evidence-grade recoverability, attacked-backup assumptions, service delivery, sovereignty spread, and surface expansion are safe planning assumptions for every participant. The structural uncertainty is concentrated in three questions — who owns the budget, who owns the base layer, and who is allowed to operate where — and each participant should identify which of the three most threatens or favors its position, then instrument the corresponding signposts. The final section converts this outlook into concrete moves for both audiences.
