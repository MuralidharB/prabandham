Chapter 8 of 11

Buyer Behavior & Commercial Models

As-a-service delivery, consumption pricing, and the evaluation criteria that actually differentiate.

Key facts
  • BaaS/DRaaS bundles three distinct value propositions — offload, elastic recovery, expertise — each with its own diligence
  • Cyber-resilience components are increasingly funded from security budgets, not infrastructure
  • Consolidation wins by default, not universally: keep deliberate, justified exceptions
  • The criteria that differentiate are the ones requiring demonstration — timed restores, compromised-admin exercises

The preceding sections traced how technology and threat forces are reshaping what data protection must do. This section examines the demand side: how enterprises actually buy, what they are willing to pay for, and how commercial models are adapting. The short version is that the purchase has become bigger, more senior, and more evidence-driven — and that pricing models are migrating from capacity owned to outcomes consumed, with friction on both sides of the table.

8.1The Shift to As-a-Service: BaaS, DRaaS, and What Is Really Being Bought

Backup-as-a-Service and DR-as-a-Service have moved from mid-market convenience to mainstream enterprise options — public trackers place the global DRaaS market alone in the mid-teens to low-twenties of billions of dollars for 2026, with growth forecasts clustering from the mid-teens to above 20% annually, several multiples of the broader data-protection market’s rate1 — but the label conceals several distinct value propositions that buyers should separate:

Operational offload — someone else runs the backup infrastructure, patches the software, and manages capacity. This is the core BaaS appeal, and it has strengthened as skilled infrastructure staff have become scarcer and as backup platforms have become security-critical systems requiring their own hardening and monitoring (a burden many IT teams quietly under-resource).

Elastic recovery capacity — the distinctive DRaaS proposition. Standby data centers priced for a disaster that may never occur are the classic DR economic problem; cloud-delivered recovery converts that fixed cost into contingent cost. Buyers pay ongoing for replication and reserve, and burst into paid compute only at test or declaration time.

Expertise on demand — managed detection on backup telemetry, recovery rehearsal support, and incident-time recovery management. In the ransomware era this is frequently the decisive component: organizations discover mid-incident that owning a backup product is not the same as knowing how to execute a clean, forensically sound, large-scale recovery under pressure.

The diligence items are equally distinct. Operational offload raises the sovereignty and access questions of Section 5 (who operates, from where, with what access). Elastic recovery raises the declaration fine print: recovery-time commitments at contracted scale, concurrency limits when a regional event hits many customers of the same provider simultaneously, and the cost of an actual declaration versus a test. Expertise raises verifiability — named capabilities and rehearsed runbooks versus a support queue. Concentration risk also returns here: a DRaaS provider is precisely the kind of critical third party that operational-resilience regulation now requires enterprises to assess and, in some regimes, report.

8.2Pricing Migration: From Capacity Owned to Consumption — and Its Discontents

Commercial models are moving along a familiar arc: perpetual licenses to subscription, subscription to consumption metrics — per protected workload, per front-end terabyte, per user for SaaS-application protection. The direction is set, but the friction is instructive.

Consumption pricing aligns cost with estate size and removes the periodic true-up negotiation, which buyers broadly welcome. What buyers increasingly resist is metric opacity and growth exposure: front-end-terabyte pricing on data that compounds double-digit annually is a built-in escalator; per-workload pricing invites definitional disputes as workloads fragment into containers and functions; and cross-charging cloud infrastructure costs through the vendor obscures the split between software value and pass-through storage. Procurement teams have adapted — multi-year growth caps, metric definitions in contract schedules, and benchmark clauses are now standard asks in large deals.

Budget ownership is shifting in parallel, as Section 4 anticipated. Cyber-resilience components — immutable vault capacity, clean-room environments, detection features — are increasingly funded partly or wholly from security budgets rather than infrastructure budgets. This matters commercially: security budgets are growing faster, tolerate premium pricing for risk reduction, and answer to board-level ransomware concern. Vendors have responded by packaging security-adjacent capabilities as separately priced tiers; buyers should recognize the pattern and evaluate whether tier boundaries track genuine cost or simply track the deeper pocket. The practical buyer counsel is to bring both budget owners to the evaluation from the start — retrofitting a security business case onto an infrastructure procurement, or vice versa, reliably delays deals by quarters.

8.3Consolidation Pressure: Platform Versus Best-of-Breed

The average enterprise runs multiple protection products — a legacy of the silo accumulation described in Section 3 — and tool consolidation is now among the most commonly cited data-protection initiatives. The platform pitch is strong: one policy engine, one recovery orchestration layer, one audit surface, one vendor relationship, and a lower aggregate operational burden. Sections 3 through 7 have each reinforced why consistency across surfaces has strategic value.

But the honest analysis is that consolidation wins by default, not universally, and buyers should know when best-of-breed still earns its complexity:

  • Frontier workloads. Where a platform’s coverage of a surface is a checkbox rather than a capability — Kubernetes application-consistency, vector-store coherence, a specific SaaS application’s full data model — a specialist tool protecting a critical workload well beats a platform protecting it nominally.
  • The isolation argument. A monoculture protection estate is itself a risk concentration: one compromised platform, one exploited vulnerability, one bad update touches everything. Some security-driven architectures deliberately retain a second, independent product for the copy of last resort precisely to break common-mode failure.
  • Regulatory segmentation. Sovereign or classified enclaves sometimes mandate tooling that the global platform standard cannot enter, making a two-standard estate the compliant design rather than a failure of discipline.

The pragmatic pattern emerging in large enterprises is consolidation with intent: a primary platform covering the broad estate, plus a deliberately chosen, deliberately small set of exceptions — each with a written justification and a review date. For vendors, the corresponding insight is that displacement deals are won on migration credibility as much as capability: the cost, duration, and risk of moving decades of retention data off an incumbent is the real moat, and platforms that industrialize migration (automated policy translation, staged cutover, incumbent-format restore) convert the moat into an on-ramp.

8.4Evaluation Criteria That Actually Differentiate

RFP templates in this market are notoriously commoditized — every vendor supports every checkbox. The criteria that separate platforms in practice are the ones that require demonstration rather than attestation:

Recovery at scale, timed. Not “supports instant recovery,” but a witnessed restore of a realistic estate slice — hundreds of VMs, a full application with dependencies — with the clock running. Performance at demo scale and performance at incident scale routinely differ by an order of magnitude.

Restore validation as a product feature. Automated, scheduled recovery testing with evidence artifacts — because regulation increasingly requires proving recoverability (Section 4.4), and because manual DR testing is the control most often skipped under operational pressure.

Compromised-scenario recovery. A tabletop or live exercise assuming the backup administrator’s credentials are hostile: can the platform’s immutability, approval gates, and isolated copies actually withstand an insider-grade attack on itself?

Day-two operability. Policy drift reporting, protection-gap detection (what exists in the estate but is not protected), and API completeness — the qualities that determine cost of ownership in year three, invisible in a proof of concept.

Buyers who restructure evaluations around demonstrated recovery rather than feature attestation report materially different vendor rankings than checkbox RFPs produce — which is itself the strongest evidence that these are the criteria that matter.

8.5Section Takeaways

Demand-side dynamics reward vendors who sell outcomes and prove them: recovery capacity that holds at declaration scale, pricing whose growth exposure is transparent, consolidation paths whose migration risk is industrialized, and features that survive a witnessed test. Buyers hold more leverage than commoditized RFPs suggest — the levers are demonstration-based evaluation, contractual growth caps and recovery commitments, dual-budget sponsorship, and consolidation with deliberate exceptions rather than platform absolutism.
