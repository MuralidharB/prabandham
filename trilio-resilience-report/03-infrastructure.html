The Shifting Infrastructure Landscape

Hyperscalers, hybrid estates, Kubernetes, SaaS and edge — the fragmenting protection surface.

Key facts
  • The protection surface now spans clouds, on-prem, Kubernetes, SaaS apps and edge — no single tool’s assumptions hold across all five
  • Hyperscaler-native services are a floor, not a strategy: they stop at their own platform’s edge
  • “Stateless” containers didn’t survive production — Kubernetes state is now recovery-critical
  • Restoring to a different environment than the source has become a core DR pattern

The unit of protection has changed. For two decades, backup and disaster recovery were designed around a stable object: the server, then the virtual machine. Today the protection surface spans hyperscaler regions, on-premises estates, container platforms, SaaS applications, and edge sites — and no single architectural assumption holds across all of them. This section examines how that fragmentation is reshaping both buyer requirements and vendor product strategy.

3.1Hyperscalers: Native Services Versus Third-Party Ecosystems

Each major hyperscaler now offers first-party backup and disaster recovery services covering its own compute, storage, and database offerings. These services are attractive on the surface: deep integration, consumption pricing, and no separate vendor contract. For organizations running predominantly in a single cloud, native tooling increasingly covers the baseline.

The limits appear at the edges. Native services are, by design, scoped to their own platform. They rarely provide consistent policy management across clouds, offer limited application-consistent protection for workloads the hyperscaler does not own, and treat on-premises assets as an afterthought. Recovery testing, long-term retention economics, and cross-account or cross-region isolation vary widely in maturity. Third-party platforms differentiate precisely here: a single control plane, uniform policies, and recovery workflows that do not assume the primary cloud is healthy — a critical distinction in ransomware scenarios where the cloud account itself may be compromised.

Egress economics remain a structural factor in architecture decisions. The major providers all waived egress fees in 2024 for customers exiting a platform entirely — a move driven largely by the EU Data Act, which requires switching charges to be phased out by 2027 — but the waivers are conditional (account closure or full workload removal, typically within a 60-day window) and one-way. Day-to-day cross-cloud replication and restore traffic still carries cost, with internet egress at the big three hyperscalers running on the order of $0.09–0.12 per gigabyte before volume discounts. Buyers designing cross-cloud DR must model egress into total cost of ownership; vendors that reduce cross-cloud data movement through deduplication, incremental-forever replication, or in-cloud recovery orchestration hold a genuine economic advantage.

Buyer implication: treat native services as a floor, not a strategy. Map which workloads are adequately covered natively and where a third-party layer is needed for cross-platform consistency, isolation, or recovery assurance.

Vendor implication: competing head-on with native services on single-cloud basics is a losing position. Durable differentiation lies in cross-platform policy, compromised-account recovery, and cost-efficient data mobility.

3.2Hybrid and Multi-Cloud Realities

Surveys consistently show that the large majority of enterprises operate hybrid estates, and repatriation of selected workloads — driven by cost predictability, performance, or sovereignty — has kept on-premises infrastructure strategically relevant rather than residual. The consequence for data protection is that “cloud-first” and “on-prem legacy” tooling silos are no longer tenable.

Three problems dominate hybrid protection:

Data gravity. Large datasets resist movement. Protection architectures increasingly follow the data rather than centralizing it — local snapshots and fast restore close to the workload, with tiered copies in object storage for retention and isolation.

Policy consistency. When the same application tier runs in two clouds and a data center, buyers expect one policy, one compliance report, and one recovery runbook. Fragmented tooling turns audits and recovery tests into manual reconciliation exercises, and auditors are noticing.

Portability and recovery mobility. The ability to restore a workload to a different environment than it came from — on-prem to cloud, cloud to cloud — has moved from a nice-to-have to a core DR pattern, both for economics (recover into cheap capacity) and for resilience (recover away from a compromised platform).

3.3Stateless Versus Stateful Workloads: The Kubernetes Reckoning

The early container era carried a convenient assumption: containers are stateless, so there is nothing to back up — redeploy from the registry and move on. That assumption has not survived contact with production. Databases, message queues, ML pipelines, and vector stores now run on Kubernetes with persistent volumes, and the platform’s own state — etcd, custom resources, secrets, RBAC configuration — is itself recovery-critical.

Kubernetes-native protection differs fundamentally from VM-era backup. The recovery unit is not a machine image but an application: a namespace or label-selected set of resources plus their persistent data, restored in the right order with the right dependencies. Crash-consistent volume snapshots alone are insufficient for transactional workloads; application-consistent capture requires orchestration hooks. Vendor maturity here varies enormously — this is one of the clearest capability gaps between archetypes examined in Section 9.

For buyers, the practical test is simple: can the platform restore a complete application, to a different cluster, on a different infrastructure, and prove it in a scheduled test? Many tools that check the “Kubernetes support” box cannot.

3.4The Expanding Protection Surface: SaaS and Edge

Two further fronts complete the picture.

SaaS application data. The shared-responsibility model is now widely understood: the SaaS provider protects the service, not the customer’s data within it. Accidental deletion, malicious insiders, ransomware that reaches synced data, and retention obligations all drive demand for third-party protection of productivity suites, CRM platforms, and identity systems. Identity-provider configuration in particular has emerged as a recovery-critical dataset — losing it can lock an organization out of everything else. SaaS backup has become a standard line item in enterprise data-protection RFPs and a growth engine for vendors.

Edge and distributed sites. Retail, manufacturing, logistics, and healthcare push compute to hundreds or thousands of small sites with no local IT staff and constrained bandwidth. Protection here demands centralized policy with autonomous local operation, tolerance for intermittent connectivity, and hardware-light footprints. Edge estates are also a soft target in ransomware campaigns — often the least monitored and least patched part of the environment.

3.5Section Takeaways

The infrastructure landscape has fragmented faster than protection tooling has consolidated. The strategic consequence cuts both ways: buyers face genuine risk of accumulating four or five disconnected protection silos (native cloud tools, VM backup, Kubernetes tooling, SaaS backup, edge point products), while vendors face a market that increasingly rewards breadth of consistent coverage over depth in any single environment. The platforms winning consolidation deals are those that can demonstrate one policy engine, one recovery orchestration layer, and credible coverage across all five surfaces — a bar that, as Section 9 shows, no archetype fully clears today.
