Storage & Technology Foundations

Media economics, capture mechanisms, and the real round-trip cost of retention.

Key facts
  • Object storage has displaced purpose-built appliances as the default backup repository
  • Flash is bought for restore speed; tape persists as the only physical air gap
  • Replication faithfully replicates corruption — availability and integrity need separate copies
  • Retention TCO is a round trip: the cost to restore at incident scale, not just to store

Strategy discussions in this market tend to orbit threats and clouds, but the economics and physics of storage still determine what is actually achievable. Recovery-time promises, retention costs, immutability guarantees, and sovereignty architectures all rest on choices about media, data-reduction technology, and capture mechanisms. This section grounds the report’s strategic themes in those foundations — and in the trade-offs that marketing materials on both sides tend to blur.

6.1The Media Landscape: Object, Flash, Disk, and Tape

Object storage has become the center of gravity for backup data. Its attributes map almost perfectly to modern protection requirements: effectively unlimited scale, native versioning, per-object immutability (object lock), lifecycle tiering, and an API model every backup platform now speaks. Whether delivered as hyperscaler storage, on-premises object systems, or specialist S3-compatible clouds, object storage has displaced purpose-built backup appliances as the default repository architecture. The competitive consequence is notable: repository economics increasingly belong to storage providers rather than backup vendors, pushing backup platforms up-stack toward orchestration, security, and intelligence for their differentiation.

Flash has crossed into secondary storage. Dense QLC flash has narrowed the price gap with disk enough that all-flash backup targets are no longer exotic. The case is rarely about backup speed — it is about restore speed. Ransomware recovery at scale means rehydrating hundreds of terabytes under executive scrutiny; flash-based repositories can cut restore windows dramatically and enable patterns like instant-mount recovery, where workloads run directly from the backup repository while data copies back in the background. Buyers paying flash premiums should tie them explicitly to RTO requirements rather than accepting them as general modernization.

Disk remains the workhorse of the middle tier — capacity economics still favor it for bulk retention that must stay warm — but its share is being squeezed from both sides by flash performance and object-storage economics.

Tape refuses to exit. Written off for two decades, tape persists because it offers the only physical air gap, the lowest cost per petabyte at scale, strong media longevity, and zero energy draw at rest. The ransomware era has rehabilitated it: a tape in a vault is unreachable by any compromised credential. Its constraints are equally real — slow, operationally heavy, sequential recovery — which confines it to the copy-of-last-resort and deep-archive tiers rather than operational recovery. For sovereignty-constrained and highly targeted sectors, tape’s role is durable.

6.2Capture Mechanisms: Matching Technology to RTO/RPO

The industry offers a spectrum of capture technologies, and the perennial buyer error is paying for a tighter recovery point than the workload justifies — or assuming a nightly backup protects a system whose tolerance for data loss is measured in minutes.

Traditional scheduled backup (incremental-forever with periodic synthetic fulls) delivers recovery points measured in hours and remains entirely adequate for the long tail of workloads. Its virtues are cost, simplicity, and — critically — version depth: a rich history of recovery points reaching back weeks or months, which is precisely what ransomware recovery requires when the last clean copy predates detection by days.

Storage snapshots deliver near-instant capture and very fast rollback within the same storage system. They are the first line of operational recovery but are not, by themselves, backup: they typically share the fate of the array, and snapshot deletion is a standard step in backup-targeting attacks. Modern architectures pair frequent snapshots (minutes-level RPO, fast RTO) with independent backup copies for isolation and depth.

Replication and continuous data protection (CDP) serve the top tier: journaled, near-zero RPO with recovery to any point in time, or synchronous/asynchronous replication for site-level failover. These technologies power true disaster recovery — but replication faithfully replicates corruption and encryption too, which is why replication-only DR strategies failed publicly in early ransomware incidents. The lesson is now orthodoxy: replication for availability, point-in-time copies for integrity, and both are required.

The strategic pattern across the market is tiered protection by workload class: a small set of revenue-critical systems on CDP or frequent snapshots with orchestrated failover; a broad middle on snapshot-plus-backup; a long tail on standard backup with immutable retention. Buyers should demand that platforms support this tiering under one policy engine — running three products for three tiers recreates the silo problem of Section 3.

6.3The Economics of Retention: Data Reduction, Tiering, and Real TCO

Retention windows are lengthening — driven by compliance, litigation readiness, and the ransomware reality that clean copies may need to reach back weeks — at the same time that data volumes compound. The gap between the two is absorbed by data-reduction technology and tiering, and this is where advertised economics and delivered economics diverge most.

Deduplication and compression ratios are workload-dependent to a degree vendor headline figures obscure: virtual-machine estates with high redundancy reduce dramatically; pre-compressed, encrypted, or media-heavy data barely reduces at all. Two structural pressures are eroding average ratios across the industry: pervasive encryption of data at source (encrypted data does not deduplicate) and the growth of already-compressed formats. Buyers should insist on ratio commitments tested against their own data profile, and should note a security trade-off rarely surfaced: aggressive global deduplication concentrates risk (many restore chains depending on shared blocks) and can slow large restores through rehydration overhead.

Tiering and lifecycle policies move aging recovery points to progressively cheaper storage classes. The trap is on the way back out: archive-class storage carries retrieval fees, restore latencies of hours, and egress charges that can make a large-scale recovery from deep archive shockingly expensive precisely when the organization is least able to negotiate. The real TCO of retention is therefore a round-trip calculation — cost to store plus cost to restore at the volumes a worst-case scenario implies. Sophisticated buyers now model a full-estate restore as a costed scenario during procurement; it routinely changes the tiering design.

Verification is the hidden line item. Immutable, tiered, deduplicated retention is worthless if the copies cannot be restored. Automated restore testing, integrity verification, and recovery rehearsal consume compute and staff time that TCO models routinely omit — and that regulation (Section 4.4) increasingly mandates. Platforms that automate verification convert a labor cost into a product capability, which is one of the quieter but more decisive differentiation axes in current evaluations.

6.4Section Takeaways

Storage foundations set the ceiling on every strategic ambition in this report: immutability guarantees are only as strong as their enforcement layer, RTO promises are only as fast as the repository media and restore path, and retention strategies are only as affordable as their round-trip economics. For buyers, the discipline is tiering — matching capture technology and media class to workload criticality under a single policy engine, and costing recovery, not just storage. For vendors, the ground is shifting up-stack: as object storage commoditizes the repository, durable differentiation migrates to orchestration, verification, security integration, and intelligence — the layers examined next in Section 7.
