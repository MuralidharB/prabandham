The Threat Landscape as Market Driver

Ransomware targets backups first. The architecture and the buying criteria have changed to match.

Key facts
  • Mature ransomware operations locate and neutralize backups before detonating
  • Backup fully solves only the encryption leg of modern extortion
  • Immutability has moved from differentiator to table stakes — but enforcement models are not equivalent
  • Insurance and regulation have shifted the bar from having backups to proving recoverability

For most of its history, data protection was purchased against accidents: hardware failure, human error, fire, flood. Those risks have not disappeared, but they no longer drive the market. Ransomware has become the primary reason enterprises re-evaluate, re-architect, and re-budget their backup and recovery estates — and it has changed what “good” looks like. This section examines how adversary behavior is reshaping product requirements, buying criteria, and organizational ownership of resilience.

4.1Ransomware’s Evolution: Backups Are Now the Target

The decisive shift of the past several years is that attackers stopped treating backups as an obstacle and started treating them as an objective. Mature ransomware operations routinely spend days or weeks inside an environment before detonation, and a standard step in that dwell time is locating and neutralizing recovery capability: deleting backup jobs, expiring retention policies through compromised admin consoles, encrypting backup repositories, and destroying snapshots. An organization that cannot restore is an organization that pays. The pattern is now quantified: the most widely cited vendor research finds backup repositories targeted in roughly 96% of ransomware attacks, with about three-quarters of those attempts at least partially successful, and ransomware was present in 44% of all analyzed breaches in the most recent Verizon DBIR — up from 32% a year earlier.1

Extortion economics have layered on top. Double extortion — encrypting data while also exfiltrating it and threatening publication — means that even a flawless restore does not end the incident. Triple-extortion variants add pressure through DDoS, direct harassment of customers and partners, or regulatory-disclosure threats. Notably, the share of victims who pay has fallen to record lows — roughly a quarter to a third, by the major trackers — a decline attributed in significant part to improved backup and recovery capability, which is the market’s clearest evidence that resilient architecture changes extortion economics.1 The strategic consequence for this report’s scope is important to state plainly: backup fully solves only the encryption leg of modern extortion. The adversary’s own adaptation confirms the point — extortion-only attacks that skip encryption entirely tripled to roughly 12% of incidents in 2025, a direct response to improving recovery capability.1 Vendors that overclaim “ransomware protection” invite buyer skepticism; buyers should understand recovery as necessary but not sufficient, one pillar of a broader resilience posture alongside detection, identity security, and incident response.

A further evolution deserves attention: attacks on the identity and management plane. Compromising a directory service or a backup platform’s own administrative credentials can be more damaging than encrypting production data, because it undermines the trust anchor on which recovery depends. This has elevated requirements such as multi-factor and multi-person authorization for destructive actions, separation of backup infrastructure from production identity domains, and protection of the identity system itself as a first-class recovery dataset.

4.2Immutability, Air Gaps, and Isolated Recovery

The market’s answer to backup-targeting attacks is architectural: make at least one copy of the data impossible to alter or delete, even by an administrator, even under duress.

Immutability has moved from feature to table stakes. Object-lock storage, write-once retention modes, and hardened repository appliances all deliver a version of the same promise: within the retention window, no credential can destroy the copy. The buyer’s diligence question is where immutability is enforced — genuine storage-layer enforcement versus a software flag the same platform can override. Not all “immutable” checkboxes are equivalent, and sophisticated buyers now ask for the enforcement model in writing.

Logical air gaps and cyber vaults extend the idea: a copy that is network-isolated, on separate credentials, in a separate failure and trust domain, with data flowing in through tightly controlled one-way processes. Tape retains a niche precisely because its air gap is physical. The design goal in all cases is the same — an adversary with full control of production and primary backup infrastructure still cannot reach the copy of last resort.

Isolated recovery environments and clean rooms address the other half of the problem: where do you restore to when production is a crime scene? Restoring compromised systems back into a compromised network re-detonates the incident. Clean-room recovery — standing up an isolated environment, restoring candidate data into it, scanning and validating before promotion — has moved from advanced practice to expected capability in enterprise evaluations. Recovery-point scanning (checking backups for dormant malware and indicators of compromise, and identifying the last known-clean copy) is among the fastest-maturing product areas in the market.

4.3The Backup–Security Convergence and the Ownership Question

These developments are collapsing the wall between data protection and cybersecurity. Backup platforms now ship anomaly detection (entropy changes, mass deletions, unusual access patterns), threat scanning, and integrations that feed security-operations tooling. Security platforms, from the other direction, increasingly treat recoverability as part of the incident-response story. Section 9 examines the “security-converged” vendor archetype this has produced.

The convergence creates an organizational question buyers must answer internally: who owns cyber resilience? Backup budgets and operations traditionally sit with infrastructure teams; ransomware risk sits with the CISO; recovery decisions during an incident sit with both, plus legal and executive leadership. In practice, purchasing decisions increasingly require joint sign-off, and RFPs reflect security-team requirements — SOC integration, forensic support, reporting for regulators — that infrastructure-led evaluations of a decade ago never contained. Vendors that can speak credibly to both buying centers hold an advantage; those that pitch only storage efficiency to an audience worried about extortion do not.

4.4Compliance and Cyber Insurance as Forcing Functions

Two external forces are converting best practice into hard requirement.

Cyber insurance underwriting has tightened substantially. Questionnaires now routinely probe for immutable or offline backups, tested recovery procedures, MFA on backup administration, and segmentation of backup infrastructure. Weak answers translate directly into higher premiums, coverage exclusions, or declined policies. For many mid-market organizations, the insurance renewal — not an internal risk assessment — is the event that triggers a data-protection modernization project. Vendors have noticed, and “insurance-readiness” framing appears throughout market messaging.

Regulation is moving in the same direction, with operational-resilience regimes in financial services requiring demonstrable recovery testing within defined tolerances, and critical-infrastructure directives imposing incident-reporting and resilience obligations across a widening set of sectors. (Section 5 treats the regulatory landscape, including sovereignty-driven requirements, in depth.) The common thread is a shift from having backups to proving recoverability — documented tests, measured recovery times, and evidence trails. This favors platforms with built-in recovery testing, reporting, and audit artifacts, and it is quietly making “we test DR annually” an inadequate answer.

4.5Section Takeaways

The threat landscape has inverted the industry’s value proposition. Backup was once insurance against rare accidents, evaluated on cost per terabyte; it is now an active security control, evaluated on whether the organization can survive a targeted, intelligent adversary who has specifically planned for its existence. Buyers should evaluate every architecture decision through the assumption that backup infrastructure will be attacked. Vendors should recognize that the buying center has shifted, the evidence bar has risen, and the fastest-growing requirements — clean-room recovery, threat scanning, insurance and regulatory reporting — sit at the intersection of data protection and security operations rather than inside either discipline alone.
