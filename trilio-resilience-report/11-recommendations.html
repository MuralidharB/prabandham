Chapter 11 of 11

Recommendations

Concrete moves for enterprise buyers and vendor strategy teams.

Key facts
  • Eight moves for buyers, eight for vendors — each traceable to the report’s analysis
  • Buyers: design for the attacked-backup assumption and procure on demonstration, not attestation
  • Vendors: fix the gap that kills you, not the gap that embarrasses you
  • Both sides converge on one discipline: proof

This closing section converts the report’s analysis into moves. It is deliberately opinionated where the analysis supports opinion, and deliberately conditional where Section 10’s scenarios leave the ground genuinely open. Part 11a addresses enterprise buyers; Part 11b addresses vendor strategy teams. Both parts assume the baseline trends of Section 10.1 as planning certainties.

11aFor Enterprise Buyers

1. Rebuild the program around the attacked-backup assumption. Design every layer as if backup infrastructure will be specifically targeted by an adversary holding administrative credentials — because it will be (Section 4.1). In practice: storage-enforced immutability with the enforcement model verified in writing, at least one copy in a separate trust domain with separate identity, multi-person authorization for destructive actions, and backup infrastructure removed from the production identity plane. Treat any component that fails the hostile-administrator test as unprotected.

2. Tier the estate; refuse uniform protection. Uniform policies overprotect the long tail and underprotect the crown jewels simultaneously. Classify workloads by recovery criticality and by regulatory constraint (Sections 5.3, 6.2), then match capture technology, media, and sovereignty posture to each tier — CDP or frequent snapshots with orchestrated failover for the revenue-critical few; snapshot-plus-immutable-backup for the middle; standard backup for the tail. Demand that the tiers run under one policy engine, or you are rebuilding the silo problem you set out to solve.

3. Buy recovery, and make vendors prove it. Restructure evaluations around demonstration rather than attestation (Section 8.4): a witnessed, timed restore at realistic scale; a compromised-administrator exercise against the platform itself; automated restore validation producing audit-grade evidence. Contract the outcomes — recovery-time commitments at contracted scale, declaration concurrency terms for DRaaS, growth-capped pricing metrics. The gap between demo-scale and incident-scale performance is the single most expensive discovery to make during an actual incident.

4. Cost the round trip, not the storage. Model a full-estate restore — retrieval fees, egress, rehydration time, staff hours — as a priced scenario during procurement (Section 6.3). Archive-tier economics that look excellent at rest routinely become punitive at recovery volume, and the moment of discovery is the worst possible one. The same discipline applies to consumption pricing: project the metric against your own data-growth curve before signing, not after.

5. Consolidate with intent; keep deliberate exceptions. Pursue platform consolidation for the operational and evidence benefits, but hold two classes of exception with written justification: frontier workloads the platform covers only nominally (Kubernetes application-consistency, AI pipeline assets, specific SaaS data models), and an independent, differently sourced copy of last resort that breaks common-mode failure with the primary platform (Section 8.3). Map your shortlist to the Section 9 archetypes to anticipate which gaps each candidate will ask you to accept — because every one of them will ask you to accept some.

6. Bring both budget owners in at the start. Cyber-resilience components will be part-funded by security; infrastructure will own operations. Joint sponsorship from the first requirements workshop — with the CISO organization owning the threat requirements and infrastructure owning the operational ones — is the difference between a two-quarter and a five-quarter procurement (Section 8.2), and it produces materially better requirements.

7. Get ahead of the AI estate now. Inventory training datasets, model checkpoints, vector stores, and pipeline configuration before protection policy exists for them, and tier them by the regenerate-versus-restore distinction of Section 7.2. Simultaneously, apply administrative-grade governance to any AI capability inside the protection platform: scoped permissions, approval gates on destructive actions, full audit trails, and explicit answers on where the intelligence runs and what data it sees (Sections 5, 7.1).

8. Convert compliance from burden to forcing function. The regulatory direction — proven recoverability, tested tolerances, third-party risk reporting — aligns almost perfectly with what a well-run program needs anyway (Section 4.4). Use the mandates to fund automated recovery testing and evidence tooling; use insurance questionnaires as a free maturity audit; instrument the Section 10.5 signposts relevant to your sector so architectural assumptions get revisited on evidence rather than on renewal cycles.

11bFor Vendor Strategy Teams

1. Locate yourself honestly in the archetype map — then fix the gap that kills you, not the gap that embarrasses you. Section 9.2’s capability picture implies a different survival move per archetype: incumbents must make modernization complete somewhere (one genuinely cloud-native line) rather than incremental everywhere; cloud-natives must solve sovereignty-flexible delivery before the regulated segment consolidates without them; security-converged players must either build estate breadth or embrace positioning as the deliberate second platform and price accordingly; specialists must convert operational excellence into attestable, marketable evidence. Strategy documents that list all gaps as equal priorities have not made a decision.

2. Compete above the floor. Hyperscaler-native services will keep absorbing single-cloud basics; pricing strategy should assume the floor rises every year (Sections 9.1, 10.3). Concentrate roadmap and message on what natives structurally cannot offer: cross-platform policy and recovery mobility, independent trust domains, compromised-account recovery, and compliance-grade evidence. Any revenue line that competes directly with a native service on its own platform should be treated as scheduled for erosion.

3. Claim white space with a real product, early. Section 9.3’s list — coherent AI-pipeline protection, sovereignty-flexible SaaS, restore-anywhere mobility at scale, recovery-evidence-as-compliance-product, edge-fleet economics — represents demand documented in this report that no archetype currently serves. Each is defensible precisely because it fits awkwardly into every competitor’s operating model. The AI-pipeline surface deserves particular urgency: the assets are accumulating in customer estates now, the requirements (lineage, model-corpus-embedding consistency) are definable now, and category credibility will belong to whoever demonstrates it first.

4. Make evidence the product. The single safest bet in the report is Section 10.1’s first baseline: recoverability becomes an evidence discipline. Automated recovery validation, tolerance tracking, and regulator-ready attestation should graduate from feature to flagship — priced, named, and sold to compliance stakeholders as well as operators. This is also the most durable answer to commoditization: storage can be commoditized; audit-grade proof of recoverability, integrated across a heterogeneous estate, cannot be easily.

5. Design for the sovereignty spectrum, not the sovereign checkbox. One product line spanning global SaaS to partner-operated to fully self-hosted — without capability loss — is expensive, which is exactly why it is defensible (Sections 5.4, 9.3). Choose target jurisdictions deliberately, invest in certifications as market-entry tickets, structure for white-label and partner-operated delivery, and make the metadata story demonstrable: catalogs, telemetry, and support access respecting the same boundaries as the data. Vendors that treat sovereignty as a deployment option will lose the segment to vendors that treat it as an architecture.

6. Treat channel geometry as strategy, not distribution. Marketplace presence is now mandatory but margin-hostile; MSPs are simultaneously customers, channel, and the sovereign delivery vehicle; GSIs anchor the largest transformations (Section 9.4). The recurring self-inflicted wound is channel conflict — underpricing your own MSP partners through marketplace listings. Set the economics deliberately, because in Section 10’s service-heavy futures, who delivers your technology matters as much as what it does.

7. Sell the converged buying center. Build the commercial motion for dual sponsorship — security-credible detection and forensics narratives for the CISO organization, operational depth and TCO for infrastructure — and equip sellers for both rooms (Section 8.2). Overclaiming is the specific trap: “ransomware protection” claims that ignore exfiltration invite the skepticism of security-literate buyers (Section 4.1); precision about what recovery does and does not solve is, counterintuitively, the stronger security positioning.

8. Instrument the scenarios. Assign ownership for tracking Section 10.5’s signposts — a security-platform mega-acquisition, hyperscaler cross-cloud protection, sovereignty criteria in certification schemes — with pre-agreed strategic responses rather than ad hoc reaction. The scenarios differ enough that early recognition is worth quarters of repositioning time.

11.3Closing Note

Both sets of recommendations reduce to the same underlying discipline: this market now rewards proof — proven recovery for buyers, provable differentiation for vendors — and punishes assumption. The organizations on either side of the table that internalize that shift earliest will find the coming phase of the market considerably more navigable than those still buying, or selling, checkboxes.
