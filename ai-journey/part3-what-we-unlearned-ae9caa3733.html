AI-First · Part 3 of 4 · The Playbook

What we unlearned making AI central at Trilio.

Parts 1 and 2 were the argument. This is what we actually changed — the process we scrapped, the one we kept, and the two products that came out the other side.

By Murali Balcha

From theory to practice

A worldview is cheap until it costs you something. It is easy to say "AI is a reasoning partner" in a blog post. It is harder to reorganize a company around the claim — to change how you assign work, what you measure, and what you are willing to throw away. We did that at Trilio, and I want to show you the specifics, because the specifics are where the argument either holds or falls apart.

Everything below rests on one shift that took me longer to accept than it should have. So let me start there.

The idea underneath everything

The economics of software just inverted

For my entire career, code was expensive. It took skilled people a long time to produce, so we treated it as the asset. We protected it. We avoided rewrites. We accumulated it and guarded it and let sunk cost quietly steer our decisions, because throwing code away meant throwing away money and months.

That premise is gone. Code is now cheap to produce and cheap to regenerate. And once code is cheap, it stops being the asset — it becomes disposable. What is durable, what is actually worth protecting, are the two things code is generated from and measured against: the specification (what to build) and the test suite (proof it works).

Flip which layer is precious, and almost every old rule about how to build software has to change.

The loop that replaces the old rules

Spec → generate → test hard → keep or discard → repeat. Every provocation later in this piece is just a consequence of taking that loop seriously — and of admitting that most of our habits were built to protect a layer that is no longer expensive.

Proof · one

The project we would not have attempted

Someone on the team pointed to an open-source project, written in Rust, that showed how to stream a disk image straight into a qcow2 file on S3. The performance was compelling — but it was a demo, missing everything that would make it a real product: sparseness, overlays, encryption, deduplication, chunking. And Trilio had exactly zero Rust expertise.

None of that stopped us. One engineer took it from proof of concept to a shipping product — adding all of the missing functionality, in a language we had never used, on a problem that demanded deep knowledge of disk-image layout and encryption. Some of the hardest systems work we have ever done, carried largely by one person with a tireless collaborator.

~6 moPOC (Dec) to release (Jul)
0prior Rust experience
~4×faster backups than the old path
A company my size would not have ventured it, if not for AI.
— the feasibility half of the thesis, in one line

This is the point I most want to land. AI did not make an existing project go faster. It changed which projects were possible for a company our size at all. When ambition gets that much cheaper, the interesting question stops being "can we afford to build this?" and becomes "why aren't we building more?"

Proof · two

The rewrite we simply did

Trilio Site Recovery for OpenShift Virtualization — which we demonstrated at Red Hat Summit — was first built with all its Kubernetes controllers in Python. Once we pushed toward a production-grade product, the team judged that the Python framework was the wrong foundation: it was cron-driven, polling the workload cluster on a timer. The right model was event-driven — watchers that reconcile on every create, update, and delete — with the maturity, static typing, and performance of Go's controller-runtime behind it.

So they rewrote all of the controllers in Go. The entire controller layer, one language to another. It took about five weeks.

Before → after
~5 wkfull Python → Go rewrite
100%of controllers rewritten
cron → eventpolling to real-time reconcile
Be honest about this one

The Python framework could have been pushed to do event-driven watchers with extra plumbing. We didn't rewrite because Python was incapable — we rewrote because Go's controller-runtime was the right foundation, and the cost of switching to it had collapsed. That is the real lesson: the rewrite wasn't a heroic gamble, it was a rational Tuesday.

Price that out under the old economics. "Let's rewrite all our controllers from Python to Go" is a proposal that dies in a planning meeting — the cost is impossible to justify against everything else it would delay. Five weeks is the disposability half of the thesis made concrete: when code is cheap, reaching for the right foundation mid-flight beats defending the wrong one out of sunk cost.

The playbook

Five things we had to unlearn

Those two products did not come from better tools alone. They came from dismantling habits that quietly assumed code was still expensive and thinking was still the bottleneck. Here is what we had to let go of — and what we replaced it with.

Unlearn
Relearn
The old reflex

Lean on 30 years of experience

Let the most senior, most experienced people run AI-first teams the way they always ran teams.

What replaced it

Keep the judgment, drop the process

Architectural taste and knowing what's worth building matter more than ever. The process orthodoxy built up over decades is what does damage — when influential people lead from old instincts, it holds the whole team back.

The old reflex

Scrum, sprints, standups

Coordinate humans through ceremony because thinking and typing were the scarce, expensive resources.

What replaced it

One spec-first loop

We scrapped scrum. The unit of collaboration moved from the standup to the spec. (The exact lightweight workflow is below — it is the one thing we made non-negotiable.)

The old reflex

Bite-sized tasks

Break work into tickets: add this button, wire up that endpoint, patch this page.

What replaced it

Own a module or a product

One engineer now owns a whole feature end to end — UI, middleware, backend, testing, release. We still break work down, but at the module or product level, not the individual-task level. (See: one engineer, one storage product.)

The old reflex

Generate code, drop it into review

Let AI write it, push the diff, and let the team sort out what it means.

What replaced it

You generate it, you own it

The author owns the code and must walk the team through it, live, and answer every question. This quietly solves the thing everyone fears about AI — a codebase full of code nobody understands. If you can't explain it, you don't ship it.

The old reflex

Code is precious; protect it

Avoid rewrites. Carry technical baggage forward. Let sunk cost make the call.

What replaced it

Code is never sacrosanct

Code is the output of a good spec — regenerable, not precious. Iterate fast and throw away what doesn't work. And write test suites that penetrate deep into the functionality: a thorough test suite is only a prompt away, and it is the safety net that makes throwing code away safe.

What replaced scrum

The one process we kept

Scrapping scrum is not the same as scrapping structure. If you remove process and put nothing in its place, you get chaos, not velocity. So we kept exactly one thing — a single, deliberately lightweight workflow. This is close to verbatim what I told the team.

01Configure Claude Codeset up the repo
02Brainstorm the featureexplore approaches
03 ↻Develop specsdraft with AI
04 ↻Stakeholder reviewgather feedback
05Implement & testAI executes the spec

Steps 3 & 4 loop until every stakeholder signs off. Nothing gets implemented before approval.

The key insight is the spec loop. Teams that iterate on specs until every stakeholder signs off find that implementation becomes pure execution — the machine moves fast when the target is clear, and there is far less rework. Get the spec right and the code almost writes itself, because in a real sense it does.

What is fixed
  • One shared workflow, above
  • Specs reviewed by stakeholders
  • Approval before implementation
What is yours
  • Any plugins
  • Any skills
  • Any agents and workflows
Why it's deliberately thin

I am not interested in making us process-heavy — that would kill the flexibility that makes each engineer effective. This is the minimum structure needed to keep stakeholders engaged and collaboration real. The process defines the checkpoints, not the tools. And it isn't theoretical: it is already producing the results we want, here, with our codebase and our people.

The result we were after: alignment without overhead. Structure where it earns its keep, freedom everywhere else.
— the whole operating model in one sentence

Where this leaves us

Cheap to throw away, cheap to attempt

Look at the two products together and you can see both halves of the same shift. Site Recovery is what happens when code becomes cheap enough to throw away — an entire controller layer rewritten in five weeks because sunk cost stopped mattering. The streaming storage product is what happens when ambition becomes cheap enough to attempt — a project a company our size would never have dared, shipped by one engineer in six months.

Between them sits everything I have argued so far. Stop treating AI as a tool and start thinking out loud with it. Recognize it as a reasoning partner and rebuild your assumptions accordingly. Then unlearn the habits — the process, the ceremony, the reverence for code — that only made sense back when thinking was scarce and code was expensive. Neither is true anymore.

That is our story. But it is a small instance of something much larger — a whole discipline, and the entire ecosystem around it, being rewritten in real time. That is where the final part goes.
