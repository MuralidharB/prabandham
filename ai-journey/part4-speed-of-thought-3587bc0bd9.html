AI-First · Part 4 of 4 · The Horizon

At the speed of thought: software engineering is being rebuilt overnight.

The first three parts were my company's story. This one is bigger than any company. The discipline itself — and the entire ecosystem built around it — is transforming faster than most teams can perceive.

By Murali Balcha

Zooming out

I have spent three essays on how we changed the way we build software at Trilio — the mindset, the process we scrapped, the products that came out the other side. But step back far enough and you can see that this is not a story about one company adopting a tool. It is a story about a discipline being rewritten in real time, and an ecosystem of habits, roles, and multi-billion-dollar tools quietly losing their reason to exist.

Here is what I believe is actually happening — five shifts that, taken together, describe a different profession than the one most of us trained for.

Five shifts

What is transforming, and how fast

None of these is a prediction about some distant future. Every one of them is already underway in teams that have taken AI seriously — mine included.

1

Context is everything

The single biggest determinant of a successful outcome is no longer the quality of your code — it is how well you manage context. The right context, assembled and maintained well, is what turns a capable model into a capable teammate. Learning to curate it, protect it, and hand it off cleanly is fast becoming the core engineering skill. Get the context right and good output follows almost automatically. Get it wrong and the most powerful model in the world flails.

2

The tool ecosystem is collapsing into context

The software-engineering tools that have anchored every team for two decades are quietly becoming unnecessary. Backlogs, issue trackers, the sprawling apparatus of project management — all of it can live as context. And context can be checked in alongside the source code, version-controlled, reviewed, and diffed like everything else. One repository becomes the single source of truth for what to build, why, and where it stands. The separate systems we bolted on to coordinate humans start to look like scaffolding around a building that no longer needs it.

3

Scrum is not just redundant — it is a drag

I will say this more strongly than I did earlier in the series. Scrum is not merely unnecessary in an AI-first team; it is actively harmful. The ceremonies, the fixed cadences, the ritual coordination all assume that thinking and typing are the scarce, expensive resources — and they are not anymore. Keeping that machinery running now slows engineering down, adds overhead where none is needed, and can quietly steer a project toward failure. The honest move is to stop, not to reform it.

4

Product development at the speed of thought

When context is the source of truth, tools collapse into the repo, and ceremony falls away, what remains is a very short distance between an idea and a working product. Think it, specify it, build it, ship it — in a loop tight enough that the bottleneck becomes your own clarity about what to build. Development at the speed of thought is not a slogan. It is the recipe, and it is the thing that will separate the teams that win from the teams that merely keep up.

5

Code is not your asset — and the moat is gone

This is the one that should keep founders up at night. Code is disposable. It does not keep a company afloat, and it is no longer a moat, because the moat that code used to provide disappeared almost overnight the moment anyone could generate it in bulk. What keeps you alive is different now: rapid value-add, engaging your customers ever more closely, and widening the distance between you and your competition faster than they can close it. Your durable advantage is your ability to constantly amaze customers with differentiating features — not the lines of code that happen to implement them this quarter.

Shift 2, made concrete

One repository, one source of truth

The clearest signal of the transformation is where the truth lives. In the old world, the truth about a product was scattered: code in the repo, requirements in a wiki, the backlog in one SaaS tool, issues in another, decisions lost in chat. Keeping them in sync was a full-time coordination tax. In the new world, all of it becomes context — and context belongs with the code.

When the backlog checks in next to the code, the coordination tools become scaffolding you can remove.

The uncomfortable part

The software moat disappeared overnight

For a generation, a hard-won codebase was a defensible moat. Rebuilding it was expensive enough that incumbents could rest on it. That is over. When code can be regenerated in bulk, the code itself defends nothing — and a great deal of enterprise value that was quietly resting on "it would take years to rebuild this" evaporated the moment that stopped being true.

I do not say this to alarm; I say it because it reorders what you should invest in. If code is not the moat, then the moat is the pace at which you add value, the closeness of your relationship with the customer, and the rate at which you widen the gap before anyone can close it.

Code is not your asset. Your ability to constantly amaze your customers is.
— where durable advantage actually lives now
What to do with this

Stop defending the codebase and start compounding customer value. Pour the velocity AI gives you into features that differentiate, into shortening the loop between customer need and shipped answer, and into a pace of improvement competitors cannot match. The teams that treat code as precious will guard a moat that has already drained. The teams that treat it as disposable will spend that same energy pulling further ahead.

The test for leaders

Two questions every software company must answer

If you lead a software business, the transformation is not an abstraction to admire — it is a mirror. I think every company now has to evaluate itself honestly on two axes, and the answers are uncomfortable for a lot of incumbents.

First: do you have anything to offer customers five years out? IT is going through a dramatic transformation, and it is imperative to look hard at your portfolio and ask whether it stays relevant in the AI era. This is not only a small-company worry. In category after category, a savvy end user can now do directly with AI what they used to pay software to do, and a lean startup can deliver something deeper at a fraction of the incumbent's cost. Even entrenched market leaders are exposed — entrenchment is no longer the same as safety.

Second: how fast can you move your roadmap? The winning cadence is near-real-time delivery to customers — not a six-month or annual release cycle. If your roadmap is still measured in quarters, that is the constraint to attack first. In the new world, software delivery should be measured in weeks.

Relevance keeps you in the game; speed decides whether you win it. You need both — and the only safe corner is the top right.

How to read it

Relevance without speed gets outrun; speed without relevance is just a faster path to nowhere. Being disrupted — the bottom-left — is where entrenched incumbents quietly sit while they mistake market share for safety. The only durable place to stand is the top right: something worth offering five years out, delivered at a cadence measured in weeks.

The end of the series

A different profession than the one we trained for

Put the four parts together and the shape is clear. It starts as a personal habit — thinking out loud with the machine instead of commanding it. It becomes a mindset — treating AI as a reasoning partner rather than a tool. It becomes an operating model — spec-first, ownership-driven, ruthless about disposing of code. And it ends here, as an industry-wide transformation happening at the blink of an eye: context as the source of truth, the old tooling collapsing into the repo, ceremony falling away, products built at the speed of thought, and the code moat gone.

None of this waits for permission. It is already happening, in teams that decided to stop protecting the old way and start building the new one. The only real question left is how quickly your organization is willing to see it — and whether you would rather be the team that gets rebuilt, or the team doing the rebuilding.
