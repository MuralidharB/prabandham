AI-First · Part 5 of 5 · The Interface

Your product's next reader isn't human.

Part 4 asked whether you'd rather be the team getting rebuilt or the team doing the rebuilding. This is what doing the rebuilding actually looks like — and the horizon just moved again.

By Murali Balcha

Where we left off

The series was supposed to close at Part 4. I called it "the end" and meant it — I had made the case I set out to make, the discipline was being rebuilt in real time, and readers deserved a clean ending. Then, in the months since, the ground moved again. Not inside the codebase this time, but on the very interface where software meets the world. And once I saw it clearly, I could not leave the series where it stood.

Here is the shift. For thirty years, we wrote software for humans to consume: through screens, through docs, through prose. That reader is being replaced. Not augmented — replaced — by agents that arrive at your product not to read but to act. Everything downstream of that fact has to change, and most companies have not noticed it has already happened.

The shift, in one number

66%

Across roughly 20,000 documentation sites hosted by Mintlify, the share of traffic served to AI agents climbed from 15.2% at the beginning of 2026 to 66% by July. Six months. Not a trend line to project forward — a transition that has already happened. Cloudflare, measuring a much broader slice of the internet independently, puts the figure at roughly 57% for HTML content. Two very different sources, the same conclusion.

This is where the series' argument about "context is everything" meets its ultimate test. In Part 4, I wrote that context was becoming the source of truth. What I did not fully appreciate then was that the consumer of that context was changing too. Documentation stopped being something humans read and became infrastructure that agents query. The people using our software are increasingly not the ones talking to it.

The tell

The behavioural signals are as telling as the volumes. Human traffic follows population — Virginia, California, Oregon, and every other state proportionally. Agent traffic follows cloud regions — Virginia, California, Oregon, and almost nothing elsewhere. Humans read during working hours. Agents never dip. Humans linger on a page for seconds. Agents leave in milliseconds — they arrived to extract structured information, not to be persuaded. These are different readers with different needs. Serving them the same artifact serves neither well.

The signature move

Same knowledge, two treatments

The shift does not ask you to choose between humans and agents. It asks you to serve them differently, from a single source. The pattern has an old name: content negotiation. Same underlying content, rendered for whoever is asking. What is new is that the second reader now matters as much as the first — often more.

Here is what the same section of a product's install docs looks like when you take the shift seriously.

For the human reader

Get it running on your cluster

The product installs through a Helm chart. You'll need a cluster running Kubernetes 1.24 or later, cluster-admin access, and an object store for backup data. Most teams have it running in under fifteen minutes. If you hit a snag, support is a click away.

For the agent reader 
# install
product:        my-product
version:        4.2
install_method: helm

requirements:
  - kubernetes: ">= 1.24"
  - rbac:       cluster-admin
  - storage:    s3-compatible

command:
  helm repo add ...
  helm install ...

verify:
  kubectl get pods

on_error:      see errors.md

One source of truth. Two representations. Nothing hidden, nothing duplicated — and the agent finally has something it can actually act on.

The framework

Five things that make software agent-native

If the reader has changed, the product must change with it. Not by bolting on a chatbot. By making the entire surface an agent can perceive — documentation an agent can parse, operations an agent can execute, failures an agent can diagnose, and a reference implementation the agent can pattern-match against. These are the five moves I believe any software company must make now, and they are what Trilio is building toward across our product line.

01

Agent-readable documentation

Every human-readable page also served as clean, structured Markdown at a predictable route. A repository-level llms.txt giving agents a map of what exists and where. Content negotiation as first-class product surface — not an afterthought.

llms.txt .md routes content negotiation
02

Published skills for install and lifecycle operations

Reusable, machine-executable playbooks for the operations customers repeatedly perform: install, upgrade, configure, backup, restore. Distributed in every format customer agents actually consume — skill.md packages, MCP servers, CLI plugins — because you do not get to pick which agent your customer runs.

skill.md MCP servers CLI plugins
03

Agent-friendly APIs on the deployed product

Not a SaaS API — a discoverable, documented surface on the product an agent is running next to. Schemas published. Endpoints predictable. Naming stable. No inference required to figure out what a call does. If your product runs in the customer's environment, the agent lives there too.

OpenAPI stable contracts schema-first
04

Structured, agent-consumable errors and status

When something breaks, the response includes an error code, a category, the failing component, the likely cause, and — where possible — the remediation path. Same for health and status. Agents can act on this. They cannot act on "something went wrong; please contact support."

error taxonomies status schemas remediation hints
05

A canonical agent reference implementation

Your own agentic interaction with the product, published as the pattern others follow. For us, that is the AI Support Portal — trained on our codebase, our operational data, and our support history. Rolling out internally now so we can build confidence in how it responds; external deployment with guardrails to follow. The reference implementation is how every other deliverable comes together.

internal launch external with guardrails reference architecture

The flywheel, at a larger surface

Skills at the centre. Everything else turns around them.

Part 4 showed a flywheel: the AI support portal captures customer signal, which feeds the roadmap, which feeds engineering, which serves the customer better — the loop that made "context compounds" concrete. That flywheel is still spinning. What Part 5 adds is the flywheel one level up — the one that runs when the shift above is taken seriously across the whole software lifecycle.

At the centre sits the shared artifact everyone reads and writes: skills. Engineering writes to them. A publishing pipeline — we use Git2docs, which turns every commit into agent-friendly documentation and validates it against the code — moves them into the world. Testing runs them against the build. Customer agents execute them. The support portal detects where they break, and that signal returns to engineering as the next thing to fix. Each turn accelerates the next.

The flywheel one level up: skills as the shared artifact, every stage reading and writing to them.

Each turn accelerates the next. Better skills produce better docs. Better docs enable cleaner customer installs. Cleaner installs produce sharper portal signal. Sharper signal produces faster patches.
— the flywheel isn't a metaphor; it is the operating model

The commitment

Trilio is committing to Q1'27

Every deliverable above ships across the Trilio product line by Q1 of 2027. The date is not chosen because it is convenient. It is chosen because the window in which customers form their mental model of "which vendors are agent-native" is closing this year, and after Q1'27 we would be catching up rather than leading. I would rather set the standard than reach for it.

This is not a promise other companies need to make on the same timeline. But it is a marker I am willing to plant publicly, because the question is not whether to become agent-native — the traffic numbers already answered that — but how quickly your company decides to see it. Marking the date is how I make sure ours has decided.

A caveat, for honesty

This is not a story about Trilio being ahead. It is a story about a shift that has already happened and a small company choosing to move on it before the shift becomes conventional wisdom. Being early is not the same as being unique, and this piece is not a boast. It is an invitation. If your product ships to environments where an agent might read the docs and act on your API — and today, that is most products — the question is not if you rebuild for the agent. It is when.

The end of the series, actually

The reader changed. The only question is how long you take to notice.

The four parts before this one were about a discipline being rebuilt. Personal habit, mindset, operating model, industry shift — every step zooming out. Part 5 is the interface being rebuilt too. The people who consume your software are increasingly agents that arrive not to read but to act, and every layer of your product — from documentation to APIs to error responses — was designed for a reader who is being replaced.

Trilio has always been where its customers are. Its customers are increasingly agents. So must Trilio be. That is what "the team doing the rebuilding" looks like from the inside, and it is the answer to the question Part 4 left open.
