AI-First · Part 2 of 4 · The Mindset

AI is a reasoning partner, not a tool — and your org is built for the wrong one.

The models are extraordinary and getting better every quarter. The bottleneck is no longer the machine's ability to think. It is our willingness to think with it.

By Murali Balcha

Picking up the thread

In Part 1, I traced my own arc — from copy-pasting code out of a chat window to running a single AI session for months — and I ended on a claim I want to defend properly here. The thing that changed most over those years was not the tool. It was how I talked to it. This piece is the argument for why that distinction is the whole game.

Because here is what I keep seeing. Organizations buy the tools, hand them out, and wait for the productivity to arrive. Some of it does. But most of the potential leaks away — not because the models fall short, but because almost everyone is using a reasoning machine as if it were a vending machine.

The fallacy of easy

"It's so easy it needs no training"

The most expensive belief in the AI era is that using AI requires no learning. It is easy to start — you type, it answers — and people mistake that low barrier to entry for mastery. So companies skip the part where people actually learn to work with it, and assume the value will show up on its own.

It rarely does. In my experience, only a few people naturally become fluent — genuinely at home conversing with the machine, coaxing its best thinking out of it. For most, it does not come naturally at all. And that is not a knock on them. It is a signal that we have badly underestimated what kind of skill this is.

The gap

Easy to start is not the same as easy to master. A chessboard is easy to set up, too. The distance between "I can type a prompt" and "I can think alongside this thing" is enormous — and almost no organization is investing in closing it.

The mental model

Vending machine, or thinking partner?

It comes down to the picture in your head. Many people picture AI as a tool that responds to prompts — you press a button, it dispenses an output. So they feed it verbs. Read the logs. Commit the changes. Add a page to the UI. Every one of those is a command you could have barked at a script.

But that is not what you are talking to. You are talking to a reasoning machine — something that can hold a real dialogue with you about any complex topic in the world, weigh competing designs, argue both sides, find the flaw in your logic. It is a tireless thinker that never gets frustrated, never gets bored of your follow-up, never sighs when you ask "but why?" for the fifth time.

Same model. Two entirely different relationships — and only one of them uses what you're paying for.

Why it matters

When you treat a reasoning machine as a tool, you cap it at the ceiling of your own instructions. It can only be as good as the command you already knew how to write. Treat it as a partner and it can take you somewhere you did not know to ask for.

The scarce skill

Curiosity is the new literacy

So what separates the fluent few from everyone else? It is not prompt-engineering tricks. It is not a secret syntax. It is curiosity — the habit of asking real questions, framed with genuine intelligence, and then staying in the conversation long enough to follow the answer somewhere interesting.

When you are curious, and you can frame a question well, you unlock the machine. You stop asking it to hand you an answer and start asking it to think with you toward one. The quality of what you get out is bounded almost entirely by the quality of the question you put in.

The scarce skill was never typing the code. It is the quality of the question you're willing to ask.

Why the instinct fights us

We were trained to command machines

If conversing with AI does not come naturally, there is a reason, and it is not personal. For decades, every machine we touched rewarded commands and punished conversation. Search boxes wanted keywords. Terminals wanted exact syntax. Software did precisely what you told it and nothing more — and "nothing more" was a feature, because a machine that improvised was a machine that was broken.

We spent our whole careers learning to be precise, literal, and imperative with computers. That training runs deep. So when we sit down with something that would rather have a conversation, our hands reach for the old reflex — issue the command, get the output, move on. The reflex is learned, which is the good news: what is learned can be unlearned.

The unlearning

Fluency with AI is less about learning something new than about switching off something old — the deep habit of treating a computer as a thing that obeys rather than a thing that thinks. The people who adapt fastest are often not the most senior engineers. They are the most curious ones.

And your organization?

Built for the wrong one

Now scale that reflex up to a whole company. If individuals instinctively treat AI as a tool, organizations do it structurally. We hand engineers AI and then measure them on ticket throughput. We break work into fragments small enough to be "tasks." We run the same standups, the same sprints, the same review rituals — and drop a reasoning machine into the middle of a process designed around the assumption that thinking is the expensive, scarce, human part.

But if AI is a reasoning partner, that assumption is inverted. Thinking is no longer the bottleneck. Which means the entire shape of how we organize work — how we assign it, how we collaborate, what we treat as precious and what we treat as disposable — was designed for a world that no longer exists. Your org chart, your process, your definition of a "task": all of it quietly assumes the wrong mental model.

You can give a team the best reasoning partner ever built and still wrap it in a process that only knows how to use a tool.
— the problem Part 3 is about solving

Closing thought

The mindset shift sounds soft — "treat it as a partner, be curious" — but it is the hardest and highest-leverage change there is, precisely because it is invisible. You cannot buy it in a license or install it in a rollout. It lives in the picture people carry in their heads, and in the questions they are brave enough to ask.

Get the mindset right and the tools deliver what they promised. Get it wrong and no amount of tooling will save you. In Part 3, I will get concrete: what we actually changed at Trilio once we took this seriously — the process we scrapped, the one we kept, and the products that came out the other side.
