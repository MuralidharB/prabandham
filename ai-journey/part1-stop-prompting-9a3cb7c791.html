AI-First · Part 1 of 4 · The Arc

Stop prompting. Start thinking out loud.

I was building software with AI before coding agents existed. Here is how I went from copy-pasting code out of a chat window to running a single AI session for months — and why the real shift was never about the tool getting better.

By Murali Balcha

Where this starts

Not long ago, a single engineer at my company shipped one of the hardest pieces of software we have ever attempted — a storage subsystem in a programming language none of us had ever written, requiring deep knowledge of disk-image internals and encryption. It took him about six months. A company my size would never have attempted it at all, if not for AI.

I will tell that whole story later in this series. I open with it because it is the destination — the kind of thing that becomes possible once you stop treating AI as a faster way to type and start treating it as a way to think. But I did not arrive there in one step. I got there by changing, slowly, how I work.

Era one · The chat window

Copy, paste, test, repeat

Before there were coding agents, I was already building with AI. My workflow ran through a plain web interface to the public models — OpenAI, Claude.ai — and it looked like this: brainstorm a high-level concept with the model, break it down into well-defined tasks, ask it to generate the code for each task, then review, copy, and test that code by hand. Rinse and repeat.

It worked. I got real velocity out of it, and for exploring new ideas and approaches it was already phenomenal — I could reason through a design with something that never got tired of my questions. But the loop had a seam running down the middle of it: I was the integration layer. Every handoff — model to editor, editor to test, back to model — was a manual copy and a context reset.

Era one: fast, but the human sat in the seam of every handoff.

Era two · The agent arrives

Claude Code closed the loop — until it forgot

When I moved to Claude Code, productivity took a step change. The model could now read and write the code itself, run the tests, and iterate without me shuttling text back and forth. The seam I had been standing in was gone. For the first time, the loop closed around the machine instead of around me.

But the early models — Sonnet and the ones before it — had a ceiling, and the ceiling was memory. Context windows were small enough that a long, ambitious task would eventually run past their edge. Mid-project, the model would lose the thread: forget a decision we had made a hundred messages ago, re-solve a problem we had already solved, ask me to re-explain the shape of the system. I spent a real fraction of my time just re-establishing where we were.

The tax nobody talks about

Lost context is not a small annoyance — it is a tax on ambition. When you know the tool will forget, you keep your tasks small enough to finish before it does. You break work into fragments not because the work wants to be fragmented, but because the tool cannot hold the whole picture. The size of the project you dare to attempt gets quietly capped by the size of the context window.

Era three · The ceiling lifts

Opus 4.8 changed the shape of the work

Then came Opus 4.8, and the ceiling lifted. A million-token context window — and, just as important, the way it manages context and memory — meant the tool stopped forgetting. I could take a complex project from beginning to end without ever leaving the session, and without the low hum of worry that I was about to lose everything we had built up.

I do not mean this figuratively. I have run a single session that lasted months — executing complex tasks, moving whole pieces of functionality around the project, refactoring across the entire system — and it held the thread the entire way. The project's history was not something I had to keep reloading into the conversation. It was simply there.

The session stopped being a place I visited and started being a place I lived. The work no longer had to be cut to fit the tool's memory.
— on the jump from a small context window to a million tokens

The arc: from human-in-the-loop, to a closed loop with a memory ceiling, to a session that simply holds.

What it unlocked

The proof was a project we would not have attempted

That persistence is not a comfort feature. It is what let one engineer take on the storage subsystem I opened with — a genuinely hard problem, in Rust, a language we had no prior experience in, requiring deep understanding of disk-image layout and encryption. He carried it from proof of concept to a shipping product in about six months, largely alone.

I want to be precise about the claim, because it is the one that matters most. AI did not simply make an existing project go faster. It changed which projects were on the table at all. A problem that would have needed a specialized team we could not staff became a problem one motivated engineer could hold in his head — with a tireless collaborator that never lost the thread.

The real shift

It was never really about the tool

For a long time I told myself I was learning better tools. Faster models, bigger context, tighter loops. All true. But when I look back at the arc, the tools are not what changed most. What changed is how I talk to the machine.

Most people, when they sit down with an AI, prompt it. They issue instructions: read the logs, commit the changes. They are using one of the most capable reasoning systems ever built as if it were a command line — a thing that executes verbs. And it will do those things. But you have hired a world-class thinking partner and asked it to fetch coffee.

Prompting a tool

> read the logs
> commit the changes
> add a button to the page
# verbs for a machine to execute

Thinking out loud with a partner

> here is what I think is going wrong,
  and why — where is my reasoning
  weakest?
# a question that invites a mind

The mental model that unlocks everything

AI is a reasoning machine. It can hold a real dialogue with you about any complex topic in the world, and it is a tireless thinker that never gets frustrated with you. When you are genuinely curious, and you can frame your questions with intelligence, that is when its power opens up. The bottleneck was never the model's ability to think. It was my willingness to think with it.

That is the shift this series is about, and it is the one most organizations have not made — not because the tools are missing, but because the mindset is. In Part 2, I will make the case for it directly: why AI is a reasoning partner rather than a tool, why few of us were ever taught to converse with one, and why your organization is very likely built for the wrong one.

Closing thought

The arc from a chat window to a months-long session looks, on the surface, like a story about tools getting better. It is really a story about a habit changing. Every step made it a little more natural to stop issuing commands and start reasoning alongside the machine — and every step raised the ceiling on what I dared to build.

Stop prompting. Start thinking out loud. The rest of this series is what happens when a whole company does.
