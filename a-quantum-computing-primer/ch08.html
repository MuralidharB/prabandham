Quantum Primitives

The five reusable engines: QFT, amplitude amplification, Hamiltonian simulation, quantum walks, QSVT.

A handful of reusable engines generate nearly every known quantum speedup. Each is an instance of the pipeline; each fills the “conversion” stage differently.

1 · QFT & phase estimation

The quantum Fourier transform maps periodic phase patterns to concentrated amplitudes — it is the phase-to-amplitude converter for frequency structure. Phase estimation wraps it into a general tool: given a unitary U and an eigenstate, it writes U's eigenphase into a register, digit by digit. Shor's algorithm is phase estimation applied to modular multiplication; quantum chemistry's energy estimation is phase estimation applied to a molecular Hamiltonian.

2 · Amplitude amplification

Grover's engine, generalized: given any procedure that produces a “good” outcome with probability p, amplitude amplification boosts it to near-certainty in O(1/√p) iterations — quadratically better than the classical O(1/p) of retrying. Each iteration is oracle (phase-mark) plus diffusion (convert); the answer's amplitude grows linearly per step while classical probability would only accumulate.

3 · Hamiltonian simulation

Feynman's founding use case: make the state vector evolve under e^{−iHt} for a physical Hamiltonian H. Quantum systems simulate quantum systems natively; the classical cost is exponential. This is the primitive behind chemistry, materials, and — increasingly — the linear-algebra family of algorithms.

4 · Quantum walks

The quantum analogue of random walks: amplitude spreads over a graph and interferes, propagating quadratically faster than diffusion. Walks power search on structured spaces, element-distinctness, and Markov-chain–flavored speedups.

5 · QSVT — the unifying frame

Quantum singular value transformation applies a chosen polynomial to the singular values of a matrix embedded in a unitary. Remarkably, amplitude amplification, phase estimation, Hamiltonian simulation, and quantum walks all arise as special cases — one polynomial choice each. It is the current best answer to “what is a quantum algorithm, really?”: engineered interference expressed as a polynomial transform.
