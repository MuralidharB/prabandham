Bits vs. Qubits

The state of a classical bit is a fact. The state of a qubit is a direction.

A classical bit is a fact: it is 0 or it is 1, and asking which one it is costs nothing and changes nothing. A qubit is a different kind of object. Its state is a direction in a two-dimensional complex vector space, written as a weighted combination of the two basis states:

|ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩ ,   with |α|² + |β|² = 1

notation — teal: amplitude · violet: phase

The weights α and β are amplitudes — complex numbers, so each carries both a magnitude and a phase. The normalization constraint says the squared magnitudes must total 1, because those squares will become probabilities the moment we measure.

The Bloch sphere

Because of normalization (and because one overall phase is physically meaningless — chapter |0101⟩), every single-qubit state maps to a point on the surface of a sphere. |0⟩ sits at the north pole, |1⟩ at the south pole, and every other point is a superposition: the latitude sets the balance between |0⟩ and |1⟩, the longitude sets the relative phase between them. Gates, which we meet in Part II, are rotations of this sphere.

What a qubit holds vs. what it yields

The amplitudes are continuous — in principle a qubit's state contains unbounded information. But it is not accessible information. A measurement returns exactly one classical bit, sampled with probability |α|² for outcome 0 and |β|² for outcome 1, and it destroys the superposition in the process. This tension — an enormously rich state, a one-bit readout — is the central design constraint of the entire field, and resolving it is what quantum algorithms are for.

Keep this A qubit is not “0 and 1 at the same time.” It is a unit vector whose components are complex weights over the outcomes 0 and 1. Everything else in this book is a consequence of doing linear algebra on that vector before you look at it.
