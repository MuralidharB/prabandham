Two Algorithms in Full: Grover & Shor

One quadratic, one exponential. Both are phase-to-amplitude conversion, dressed differently.

Two algorithms, studied end to end, are worth twenty surveyed. Grover's shows the pipeline at its most transparent; Shor's shows it at its most consequential. Both are phase-to-amplitude conversion — the diffusion operator and the inverse QFT are the same idea wearing different mathematics.

Grover's search — the quadratic engine

Problem: find the one marked item among N with only oracle access. Classical cost: ~N/2 queries. Grover: ~(π/4)√N.

  1. Flatten: Hadamards put amplitude 1/√N on every item.
  2. Mark: the oracle flips the phase of the answer — magnitudes untouched, statistics still flat.
  3. Convert: the diffusion operator reflects every amplitude about the mean. The negative-phase item lands far above the others; each iteration transfers more mass to it.
  4. Repeat ~√N times, then measure. Overshooting past the optimum reduces the success probability — the rotation keeps going — so the iteration count is part of the algorithm.

Shor's factoring — the exponential result

Problem: factor N. Classical reduction: factoring → finding the period r of f(x) = aˣ mod N. The quantum part finds r.

  1. Flatten: uniform superposition over exponents x in a control register.
  2. Compute: evaluate aˣ mod N into a second register — entangling control with function values. The control register is now a superposition over a periodic comb of x-values; the period r lives in its relative phases.
  3. Convert: the inverse QFT turns the periodic phase pattern into amplitude peaks at multiples of 2ⁿ/r — frequency analysis by interference.
  4. Measure & post-process: a peak sample plus continued fractions yields r; from r, classical arithmetic extracts the factors.
Deep dive · annotated walkthrough

Shor’s Period-Finding, Annotated

The formal mechanism, with the swing-and-rhythm intuition pinned next to each step. Read the formal line, then the annotation that translates it. The single picture to hold onto throughout: a child on a swing. Pushes timed with the swing add up and it climbs high. Pushes at random times fight each other and it barely moves. Everything below is that one idea, dressed in symbols.

1 · The reduction: factoring becomes “find the loop length”

To factor a large number N, pick a random a sharing no common factor with N, and consider

f(x) = aˣ mod N

This function is periodic: there is a smallest r > 0 with aʳ ≡ 1 (mod N). That r is the period. If r is even and a^(r/2) ≢ −1 (mod N), then gcd( a^(r/2) ± 1, N ) is a nontrivial factor of N. Choose a register size Q = 2ⁿ with Q ≥ N² — the reason for that bound arrives in step 4.

In plain words

Raise a number to higher and higher powers and write down each remainder after dividing by N. The list of remainders doesn’t wander off forever; it loops, like a pattern on wallpaper. The strange fact Shor exploits: the length of that loop secretly hands you the factors.

2 · Superposition, and the collapse to a periodic comb

Prepare the control register in uniform superposition and apply the reversible modular-exponentiation unitary |x⟩|0⟩ ↦ |x⟩|aˣ mod N⟩:

(1/√Q) Σₓ |x⟩ |aˣ mod N⟩ ,   x = 0 … Q−1

This is the step people misread as “the speedup.” It is not. Yes, f is now evaluated over all x at once — but measuring here would return a single random (x, f(x)) pair, no more useful than one classical evaluation. The superposition is necessary but not sufficient: the period is encoded in the correlations across the branches, and only interference can extract it.

In plain words

Playing every possible rhythm at once is not the trick — listening to the mixture naïvely just gives you one random note. The value is that the loop structure is now written across the whole ensemble, where the next step can make it sing.

Now measure the second register (conceptually — by deferred measurement it needn’t happen physically). Observing some value f₀ = a^(x₀) mod N collapses the first register onto only the x consistent with it:

(1/√M) Σⱼ |x₀ + j·r⟩ ,   j = 0 … M−1,  M ≈ Q/r

The surviving state is a train of evenly spaced spikes: period r, random offset x₀. The information you want (r) is the spacing. The information you can’t control (x₀) is the offset. The whole trick of the next step is to read the spacing while discarding the offset.

In plain words

A drumbeat now exists: a beat every r steps, starting at some random moment. The beat spacing is the secret you’re after; the random starting moment is noise you must learn to ignore.

3 · The Fourier transform, and the interference at its heart

Apply the quantum Fourier transform to the first register:

QFT: |x⟩ ↦ (1/√Q) Σᵧ e^(2πi·xy/Q) |y⟩

Acting on the spike train, the amplitude of outcome |y⟩ becomes

amp(y) = (1/√(QM)) · e^(2πi·x₀y/Q) · Σⱼ e^(2πi·jry/Q)

left factor: the offset, a pure phase · right factor: a geometric series of unit phasors

Two observations carry the entire payoff. First, the offset x₀ appears only inside a unit-modulus phase factor — it vanishes when you square for the probability. The random offset is invisible to the measurement, exactly as required. Second, the geometric series is a sum of M unit vectors, each rotated from the last by a fixed angle θᵧ = 2πry/Q. Whether they stack or cancel depends entirely on that angle.

In plain words

This is the swing. Each phasor is one push. The question for any candidate answer y is: do the pushes land in time, or at random? And note the offset’s fate — where you begin pushing doesn’t change whether the swing builds up. Only the timing matters.

Constructive case — the answers that match the rhythm. When y is a (near-)multiple of Q/r, every phasor points the same way and they stack into a long resultant of length ≈ M:

|amp(y)|² ≈ M/Q = 1/r    at each of the r favored outcomes

These r outcomes together absorb essentially all the probability mass.

Destructive case — every wrong answer. For any other y, the phasors fan out evenly around the circle and curl back to where they started, summing to nearly zero.

In plain words

Pushes landing in time: they add up, the swing climbs high, probability piles onto exactly the outcomes that encode the loop length. Pushes at random: the swing barely moves — every wrong outcome cancels itself into silence. When you finally look, almost all the loudness sits on the rhythm you were chasing.

A clean picture for the whole transform: it is a diffraction grating. A periodic row of slits with spacing r throws sharp bright fringes at reciprocal spacing Q/r. The QFT is the reciprocal-space transform — a comb in, a comb out.

4 · Reading the loop length off one measurement

You measure once and obtain some c ≈ k·Q/r for an unknown integer k. Divide:

c/Q ≈ k/r

You hold a number very close to a simple fraction, knowing neither numerator nor denominator. The continued-fraction algorithm finds the unique simplest fraction near a given number — run it on c/Q and it snaps to k/r in lowest terms, whose denominator is (a divisor of) r.

In plain words

You heard the rhythm, but as a slightly fuzzy reading on a dial. Continued fractions is the cleanup step that rounds the fuzzy reading to the exact, clean fraction it must have come from.

This is why Q ≥ N² was demanded up front: two distinct fractions with denominators below N differ by at least 1/N², so a fine-enough Q guarantees the measured value falls unambiguously into one basin. A run can still fail — k may share a factor with r, or r may be odd — but each attempt succeeds with constant probability, so a handful of repetitions nails r.

In plain words

Record the rhythm at high enough resolution that two different rhythms can never be mistaken for each other.

5 · The deeper view: the rhythm is a pitch

There is a cleaner framing that unifies all of this with the rest of quantum computing. Define the unitary U: |y⟩ ↦ |a·y mod N⟩ — multiplication by a. Because applying U a total of r times returns you to the start (aʳ ≡ 1), its eigenvalues are e^(2πi·s/r): the eigen-phases are exactly the multiples of 1/r.

Order-finding is then just quantum phase estimation run on U. The starting state |1⟩ happens to be an equal superposition of all r eigenvectors, so a single run yields a random s, and the QFT-based phase-estimation circuit extracts the phase. The modular-exponentiation step is the controlled-U^(2ʲ) operations of phase estimation in disguise. Same circuit, two framings — period-finding is the concrete view; phase estimation shows why the same machinery powers algorithms far beyond factoring.

In plain words

The loop length is a pitch, and the algorithm is a tuner: it listens to multiplication-by-a and reports the frequency it hums at. Every multiple of 1/r is a note in that chord — one measurement picks a note, and arithmetic names the chord.

One idea, two converters

Grover's diffusion converts a single phase mark into amplitude, buying a quadratic gain. Shor's inverse QFT converts a global periodic phase pattern into amplitude, buying an exponential one. The difference in payoff is exactly the difference in exploitable structure — chapter |1001⟩'s boundary, made concrete.
