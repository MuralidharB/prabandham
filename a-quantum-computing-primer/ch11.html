The Cryptographic Threat

What Shor's algorithm actually breaks, why the clock started before the hardware exists, and what migration looks like.

Most primers end at the algorithms. This one ends at their consequences, because Shor's algorithm is not an intellectual curiosity — it is a scheduled demolition of the public-key cryptography underneath nearly all digital trust.

What breaks, and how badly

CryptographyQuantum impactVerdict
RSA, Diffie–Hellman, ECCShor solves factoring & discrete log in polynomial timeBroken outright at sufficient scale
AES, symmetric ciphersGrover halves effective key length (√ speedup only)Weakened; AES-256 remains safe
SHA-2/3 hashingGrover-type speedups onlyLarger outputs suffice

The asymmetry follows directly from Part IV: public-key schemes rest on structured problems (periodicity — exactly what the QFT converts), while symmetric primitives present only unstructured search, where the quadratic ceiling holds.

Harvest now, decrypt later

The threat clock did not start with working hardware. Adversaries can record encrypted traffic today and decrypt it when a cryptanalytically relevant quantum computer (CRQC) arrives. Any data whose confidentiality horizon exceeds the CRQC timeline — state secrets, health records, long-lived intellectual property — is effectively at risk now. Migration urgency is set by data lifetime plus migration duration, not by hardware headlines.

Post-quantum cryptography

PQC replaces the vulnerable structure: NIST's standardized algorithms — ML-KEM (Kyber) for key encapsulation, ML-DSA (Dilithium) and SLH-DSA (SPHINCS+) for signatures — rest on lattice and hash problems with no known quantum converter. These run on classical hardware; the migration is a software and inventory problem, not a hardware one.

The real bottleneck: knowing where your cryptography is

Enterprises cannot replace what they cannot find. Cryptography hides in TLS configurations, certificate estates, libraries, protocols, firmware — and, hardest of all, in hand-rolled implementations that no library-signature scanner can see. Migration therefore begins with discovery and inventory (CBOMs — cryptographic bills of materials), proceeds through prioritization by data lifetime, and lands on crypto-agility: architectures that can swap algorithms without rewrites, because this migration will not be the last.

The arc of this book, closed A qubit is a vector (|0001⟩). Phase hides information (|0101⟩). The QFT converts periodic phase to amplitude (|0111⟩–|1000⟩). Periodicity underlies RSA (|1010⟩). Therefore: inventory your cryptography before the converter is built at scale. The mathematics of Part I is the reason for the migration project in Part V.
