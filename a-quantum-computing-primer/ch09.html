Where Quantum Fits: Complexity

BQP among its neighbors — what quantum computers speed up, and what they don't.

Quantum computers do not “try everything in parallel and pick the best.” Their power is precise, bounded, and best expressed in the language of complexity classes.

BQP and its neighbors

BQP — bounded-error quantum polynomial time — is the class of problems a quantum computer solves efficiently with high probability. What is known, and what is believed:

  • P ⊆ BQP: quantum computers can do everything classical ones can, efficiently. (Reversible simulation of classical circuits.)
  • BQP ⊆ PSPACE: a classical machine with enough memory can simulate any quantum computation — slowly. Quantum computers compute nothing uncomputable; they change costs, not possibility.
  • Factoring ∈ BQP (Shor), while it is believed to lie outside P — the flagship evidence that BQP strictly exceeds classical polynomial time.
  • NP-complete problems are believed to lie outside BQP. Grover gives a quadratic — not exponential — speedup on brute-force search, and the BBBV theorem proves the quadratic is optimal for unstructured problems.

The shape of quantum advantage

The pattern from Part III explains the boundary: exponential speedups appear where the problem has algebraic structure a converter can exploit — periodicity for the QFT, physical dynamics for Hamiltonian simulation. Where there is no structure, only the generic √-speedup of amplitude amplification remains. Quantum computing is a scalpel for structured problems, not a hammer for hard ones.

Keep this Three anchors: quantum ⊇ classical (P ⊆ BQP); quantum ≤ exponential classical (BQP ⊆ PSPACE); and the interesting middle — factoring-like structured problems — is where the field's consequences, including Part V's, live.
