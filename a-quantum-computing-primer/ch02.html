Registers, Classical and Quantum

Why n qubits carry 2ⁿ amplitudes — and why you still can't read them all.

A microprocessor register is a small array of independent bits: a 64-bit register holds one 64-bit value, and describing its state takes 64 bits. A quantum register of n qubits is not an array of independent qubits — it is a single joint object whose state assigns an amplitude to every one of the 2ⁿ basis strings:

|ψ⟩ = α₀₀₀|000⟩ + α₀₀₁|001⟩ + … + α₁₁₁|111⟩

Three qubits: 8 amplitudes. Fifty qubits: about 10¹⁵ amplitudes — already past what a classical supercomputer can store exactly. The state vector grows exponentially while the hardware grows linearly. That asymmetry is the raw material of quantum advantage.

Why this is not free parallelism

It is tempting to read “2ⁿ amplitudes” as “2ⁿ parallel computations whose answers we can collect.” We cannot. Measuring the register collapses it to one basis string, sampled by the |amplitude|² rule — n classical bits out, everything else gone. The exponential state space is a workspace, not an output tape. Algorithms must choreograph interference so that, by measurement time, the amplitude mass sits on strings that encode the answer.

Contrasts worth keeping explicit

CPU registerQuantum register
State descriptionn bits2ⁿ complex amplitudes
Sub-partsIndependent bitsGenerally entangled; no individual states
CopyingTrivial (MOV)Impossible for unknown states (no-cloning)
ReadoutFree, non-destructiveOne sample, destructive

The no-cloning theorem deserves a sentence of its own: there is no operation that copies an arbitrary unknown quantum state. This is why quantum programs cannot checkpoint their state, why debugging is statistical, and — in Part V — why quantum key distribution can detect eavesdroppers.
